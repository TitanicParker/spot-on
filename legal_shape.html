The Legal Shape of the Case

A legal-forensic reading of the Spot On repository against the clinical record.

Jurisdiction: Ireland · Core period: 2017–2026 · Analytical premise: the pre-discharge Protest occurred, its omission from the travelling record is real, and the Protest created a decision fork before the authoritative account was complete.

1. Legal Position

The strongest legal theory is not “failure to diagnose dystonia.” It is that a clinically material relationship was known to remain unresolved before documentary closure; the service then completed an authoritative synthesis that gave the unresolved foot territory a separate peripheral/structural future without preserving the Protest or demonstrating the relational investigation required to earn that separation; and that information architecture governed later care despite repeated evidence crossing its boundaries.

That alleged mechanism has four principal legal dimensions: clinical negligence; professional regulation; institutional governance and hospital liability; and record/data accuracy. None requires proof of malice. The phrase reckless reconstruction is used here as a factual description, not as a standalone Irish tort or offence.

2. Assumed Factual Premise

This analysis assumes the patient protested near the end of the July 2017 admission that the foot problem which had brought him into the system remained unexplained; that the Protest was known to the responsible Neurology service; that it created a real choice whether to reopen and preserve that unresolved relationship or proceed to closure; and that the final travelling account omitted the Protest while developing a separate/peripheral explanatory route.

That assumption matters because notice changes the legal character of the conduct. Before notice, a failure may be characterised as oversight. After notice, the issue becomes what a specialist service did with known incompleteness.

3. What the Record Establishes Independently of the Premise

The GP referral presented one integrated clinical problem: bilateral foot issues, an unusual festinant and stiff gait, trouble initiating movement, increased tone and stiffness in upper and lower limbs, brisk reflexes, toe clawing, declining dexterity and worsening function. Parkinsonism was one possible underlying diagnosis, not the organising fact from which the feet had already been separated.

Neurology then objectively documented severe young-onset Parkinsonism and a strikingly abnormal gait with a lower-limb posture described as “somewhat suggestive of a dystonic posture — NOT FOOT DROP.” The contemporaneous DaTscan request also described the walk as “almost dystonically unusual” and recorded “some form of symptoms for upto 13 years,” while suspecting an autosomal-recessive Parkinsonian disorder.

The neurologist later wrote to an external specialist that the patient “seems to have had symptoms of Parkinson’s for the last 13 years” and sought genetic advice. The eventual PRKN/PARK2 molecular report describes lower-limb dystonia as a characteristic feature that can remain isolated for years. That later biology must not be projected backwards as knowledge, but it is a legitimate retrospective stress test of the explanatory priorities adopted in 2017.

4. The Protest as a Legal Fork

The critical alleged act is not silence. On the assumed facts, the service was told that the originating foot problem remained unresolved while the patient, the observed dystonic sign and the unfinished record were still available. The record then acquired a completed explanatory architecture.

The legally relevant alternatives were ordinary clinical ones: re-examine; seek movement-disorders input; preserve the relationship explicitly as unresolved; arrange a defined neurological review; articulate how Podiatry and Neurology were meant to reconnect; or proceed while recording the patient's objection and the reasons for doing so. A court or regulator would not need to find that only one option was lawful. It would ask whether the option actually taken met the standard of reasonable specialist care and honest, clinically adequate record-making.

5. Reckless Reconstruction

The discharge synthesis did not simply say “we do not know.” It developed a compact pathway: “burning”; broader “leg symptoms including corns and callouses and burning”; possible complete separation; “perhaps a secondary peripheral neuropathy”; previous alcohol exposure; “Not clear as this has improved”; the patient's phenomena being “in his own mind — markedly divorced”; and a Podiatry future.

Peripheral neuropathy was medically possible. The legal problem is the relative evidential status of the competing branches. Structural abnormalities and dystonic-looking movement were observed. Neuropathy was hypothesised. Yet the hypothesised branch acquired more explanatory and managerial infrastructure than the observed dystonic-foot relationship.

If that architecture was completed after direct notice that the feet remained unresolved, and if it functioned to permit closure while omitting the notice that closure was disputed, “reckless reconstruction” is a fair factual description of the alleged breach: conscious completion in the face of an obvious risk that the specialist record would misstate how much of the problem had actually been worked through.

This is not the same as alleging knowing falsification. Recklessness, negligence and dishonesty are different concepts. The evidence can support a serious case without pretending that private malicious intent has already been proved.

6. Clinical Negligence

The conventional civil route is negligence. The Irish standard remains governed by the Dunne principles: the question is whether the clinician acted in accordance with a practice approved by a responsible body of comparable practitioners, subject to the qualification that the practice itself must withstand logical analysis. The case therefore requires independent neurological expert evidence.

The putative breaches should be pleaded with precision: failure in 2017 adequately to investigate and preserve the observed dystonic lower-limb relationship to the painful feet after it was made a live issue; failure to communicate the true epistemic state to downstream clinicians; and later failure to undertake global reassessment when repeated neurological-foot evidence accumulated.

The claim should not be framed as “PRKN should have been diagnosed in 2017.” The stronger question is whether ordinary specialist care required the dystonic lower-limb finding and the integrated GP presentation to remain clinically active rather than being displaced by a speculative peripheral branch.

7. Professional Regulation

Under the Medical Practitioners Act 2007, poor professional performance concerns failure to meet the standards of competence reasonably expected of a practitioner practising that kind of medicine. If the conduct is proved as a sustained failure of application—observation without integration, known incompleteness followed by closure, and later failure to reconstruct despite contrary evidence—it fits that category more naturally than a single innocent error.

Professional misconduct is a more serious possibility. Irish authority does not require fraud or moral turpitude in every case; a sufficiently serious falling short of professional standards may qualify. Whether this case crosses that threshold would depend on findings about seriousness, knowledge, record-making and persistence.

8. Record Integrity

The distinctive feature is that the alleged wrong is partly informational. A medical record need not contain a literal false sentence to mislead. It can misrepresent epistemic status: turning “this relationship has not been adequately worked out” into “this relationship has been considered and can be managed through separate pathways.”

If the Protest occurred but did not travel, the record removed the contemporaneous warning that closure had been challenged. Future clinicians then received the explanatory product without the fact that its adequacy had been disputed at the time. That can change who owns a problem, what needs reopening and how later patient dissent is interpreted.

9. Hospital and Department

The hospital may face vicarious liability for negligent acts of staff and, depending on the facts, direct systems liability. The departmental issue is not that a department is itself “guilty” in the criminal sense. It is whether its governance allowed an unresolved relationship to become an authoritative pathway without a return loop, and whether continuing specialist care later failed to reconstruct the whole.

The longitudinal record is important here. It documents neurological treatment-state effects in the foot territory, Podiatry attribution of foot problems to increased tone and abnormal gait, later characterisation of apparent footdrop as Parkinsonian, OFF-dystonia reasoning, OFF-related leg pain and shuffling, incomplete relief on maximal pregabalin, and later profound relief after Sinemet CR. The issue becomes not whether each later encounter was independently reasonable, but whether the founding model remained too stable in the face of accumulating pressure against it.

10. Complaint and Governance Failure

The 2025 complaint expressly raised diagnostic anchoring and prolonged harm. QPS initially characterised the matter as solely clinical judgment and forwarded it to the treating consultant. That is potentially important because HSE complaint guidance recognises mixed complaints: even where clinical judgment is excluded from the ordinary complaint route, issues such as whether concerns were heard, taken seriously, documented or handled appropriately can remain non-clinical governance questions.

If the complaint included the omission of the Protest, the integrity of the founding record and the mechanism by which later care inherited it, an adequate governance review needed to examine those issues rather than answer only whether levodopa timing or alternative diagnoses were defensible. A later “no deficit in care” conclusion does not automatically answer a record-integrity allegation.

11. Data Protection and the Operative Record

The strongest GDPR theory is not “a historical clinical opinion was wrong, therefore erase it.” The Data Protection Commission cautions that opinions in medical records are not automatically inaccurate merely because disputed. The sharper question is whether the operative record became incomplete or misleading because material contemporaneous dissent was omitted, provenance blurred, or continuing reliance conveyed greater epistemic certainty than the underlying work justified.

Depending on the precise requests and processing events, Articles 5(1)(d), 16, 18, 19 and 24 GDPR may be engaged: accuracy, rectification/completion, restriction while accuracy is contested, notification to recipients, and organisational responsibility. These are fact-sensitive questions for the controller, DPC or court; the patient's correspondence establishes that these rights were invoked, not by itself that every alleged infringement occurred.

12. Causation and Harm

Causation is where the legal case must become granular. It is not enough to show that the record was flawed. The claimant would need expert evidence showing what probably would have happened had the feet–dystonia relationship been properly investigated or preserved, and what harm would probably have been avoided.

The record supplies potentially important damage evidence: prolonged foot pain and functional limitation; documented psychological burden and fear/attention surrounding walking; periods in which pain dominated quality of life despite high-dose pregabalin; treatment-state relationships; and later marked benefit after dopaminergic adjustment. A legal claim must separate pain caused by underlying disease from additional pain caused by delay, misallocation or failure to reassess.

13. Proof Architecture

  1. Prove the Protest and notice: witness evidence, bedside/teaching evidence, contemporaneous communications and any available institutional traces.
  2. Prove drafting chronology: native discharge drafts, dictation metadata, audit trails, timestamps and version history.
  3. Prove clinical standard: independent movement-disorders/young-onset Parkinsonism expert evidence focused on what a reasonable neurologist should have done with the observed dystonia and unresolved feet.
  4. Prove longitudinal non-reconstruction: identify each later boundary-crossing event and ask what it did to the founding model.
  5. Prove causation and damage: compare the likely counterfactual pathway with the actual pain, treatment and functional course.
  6. Prove governance scope: compare what was complained of with what QPS/PAR/SIMT actually investigated.
  7. Prove data processing: map rectification/restriction requests, decisions, later uses and recipients.
The single most important disclosure question: when, by whom and from what source did the consequential 2017 phrase cluster first enter the discharge/clinic narrative? If it substantially pre-dated the Protest from identifiable bedside evidence, the post-notice reconstruction theory narrows. If it appeared or materially changed after notice and solved the precise closure problem the patient had raised, the seriousness increases sharply.

14. What Is Not Proved

This analysis does not require proof that every corn, callus or pain episode was dystonic; that peripheral neuropathy was impossible; that PRKN should have been molecularly confirmed in 2017; or that every delay in levodopa was negligent.

Nor does the present record by itself prove fraud, conspiracy, deliberate falsification, corruption or an intention to injure. Those allegations require different evidence of dishonest or criminal states of mind.

The severe but supportable allegation is narrower: after direct notice that a clinically material relationship remained unresolved, specialist authority may have converted unfinished neurological work into an authoritative separate/peripheral management state, omitted the contemporaneous warning that closure was disputed, allowed that state to govern longitudinal care despite repeated contradictory signals, and later participated in or benefited from institutional processes that did not squarely reconstruct the founding decision.

15. Conclusion

Read with the clinical record, the repository is strongest when the alleged legal event is identified precisely. Parkinsonism was real. Structural foot disease was real. Neuropathy was a legitimate possibility. The alleged wrong is the post-notice reorganisation of an unresolved clinical relationship into a durable information structure.

If independent neurological evidence establishes that no reasonably careful comparable neurologist would have handled the observed dystonia, the Protest, the final synthesis and later contrary signals in this way, the facts can support a concrete negligence case. If the conduct represents a serious failure of professional competence, poor professional performance is engaged. If the knowing handling of incompleteness and authoritative documentation is sufficiently serious, professional misconduct becomes a live question. Hospital governance and data-protection issues then stand alongside, rather than merely underneath, the original clinical claim.

The central legal question is therefore not whether malice can be proved. It is whether specialist authority was used, after notice, to close and transmit a model whose appearance of completion exceeded the clinical work beneath it—and whether that model then caused avoidable harm.
