# The Construction of Complexity: How an Unresolved Foot–Dystonia Relationship Became a Separate Peripheral Problem

The July 2017 discharge summary and accompanying Neurology narrative are, on first reading, the record of an extraordinarily complicated case. They range across young-onset Parkinsonism, atypical movement phenomenology, genetics, toxic exposures, protein pathology, future testing, treatment strategy, prognosis and counselling. The intellectual breadth is unmistakable. So is the seriousness of the neurological diagnosis.

But breadth of discussion is not the same thing as complexity of every clinical problem contained within it.

Repeatedly stripping this record back to its source observations produces a more precise distinction. The genuinely sophisticated problem was the **subtyping of severe Parkinsonism in a 39-year-old man**, possibly with symptoms stretching back many years, with relative symmetry, little tremor and unusual lower-limb posturing. The record itself recognised a young-onset Parkinsonian syndrome while acknowledging atypical features.

A second problem, however, was much more bounded. This patient also had longstanding painful and structurally abnormal feet. He had corns and calluses, reported fallen arches, required treatment for the feet from approximately 2009–10, and said that the foot problem remained a problem. During the same admission, Neurology objectively documented pes planus and hammertoes. At the same time, the neurological examination described a “strikingly abnormal” gait and a footdrop-like appearance “somewhat suggestive of a dystonic posture — NOT FOOT DROP.”
The simplest unresolved clinical question was therefore not obscure:

> **How did these persistent painful, structurally abnormal feet relate to the objectively dystonic-looking lower limb and abnormal gait?**

The discharge synthesis did not resolve that question. More importantly, it did not merely leave it blank. It developed a different explanatory object in its place: “burning,” broader “leg symptoms,” possible complete separation, possible peripheral neuropathy, possible alcohol causation, uncertainty attributed to improvement, strengthened language about the patient's own separation of the phenomena, and ultimately a Podiatry pathway.

That transformation is the central documentary event examined here.

The contention is not that peripheral neuropathy was impossible. It was medically possible. Nor is the contention that Podiatry was inappropriate: structural foot pathology was real. The problem is subtler and more consequential. A relatively small number of direct clinical observations accumulated a much denser inferential superstructure, while an objectively observed dystonic lower limb received comparatively little development as a possible participant in the foot problem.

The result is a record whose **inferential density exceeds its evidential density** at exactly the point where the feet are converted into a separate peripheral problem.

## Three different kinds of complexity

“This was a complex case” is simultaneously true and insufficient.

The first complexity was genuine **disease complexity**. A man aged 39 had marked rigidity and bradykinesia, very little tremor, severe gait abnormality and relative symmetry. The examination documented markedly increased tone, brisk reflexes, lead-pipe rigidity, axial rigidity and gross bradykinesia. Establishing what form of very-young-onset Parkinsonism this represented was legitimately difficult.

The second question was **clinical relational complexity**: what was the relationship between the patient's painful abnormal feet and the lower-limb movement disorder Neurology could directly observe? That question might ultimately have proved difficult. But these documents do not show it becoming difficult *after* a systematic relational investigation. They show it remaining unresolved without documenting the bedside and biomechanical work that would first be expected to clarify whether the structural feet and dystonic movement were independent, interacting or causally related.

That difference matters:

**Hard after adequate investigation is not the same thing as unresolved before adequate relational investigation is shown.**

The third complexity was **documentary complexity**. This is the complexity created by the structure of the specialist narrative itself: multiple hypotheses, category shifts, causal suggestions and increasingly differentiated explanatory branches.

Much of what a later reader experiences as “complexity” around the feet belongs to this third category.

The patient did not arrive carrying a proven neuropathy plus structural foot disease plus Parkinsonism plus an alcohol-related peripheral syndrome. Those distinctions were progressively generated inside the written synthesis.

## The clinical object before the synthesis

The early history is strikingly concrete.

The patient described bodily and leg tension dating back many years. He recalled that the next issue was “corns and calluses” and the appearance of “fallen arches in both feet.” He had required treatment for the feet from approximately 2009–10, and the document states that they “remain a problem.”

Then comes an important natural experiment in his history. After leaving bar work in 2012, the **pain in the feet** diminished substantially. The record says it reduced “to a degree ‘out of the picture’.” Yet the same sentence immediately records that he continued to have “leg tension” and that “there are still corns an calluses.” He therefore began to regard the foot pain and leg tension as “not the same problem.”

Nothing about that account is inherently exotic.

It establishes several things. Pain changed with circumstances. Tension persisted. Corns and calluses remained. The patient perceived a difference between the painful foot phenomenon and the tension phenomenon.

It does **not** establish that the feet and the neurological disorder were biologically independent.

The admission then supplied objective observations that made the relational question more, not less, important. Neurology found pes planus and hammertoes. It also found gross rigidity and bradykinesia and observed that the left lower limb “kicks out like a foot drop.” Most decisively, the gait was called “strikingly abnormal,” with a footdrop-like appearance “somewhat suggestive of a dystonic posture — NOT FOOT DROP.”

At that point there were observable facts on both sides of the question:

**structural and painful feet**
and
**abnormal neurological movement of the lower limb.**

The relationship between them required explanation.

## What actually went “out of the picture”

The phrase “out of the picture” is important because its later documentary effect exceeds its literal scope.

The record does not say that the **feet** went out of the picture.

It says:

> the **pain in the feet** reduced to a degree “out of the picture”.

The grammatical subject is pain. And the surrounding text makes the limitation unmistakable. Immediately before it, the feet “remain a problem.” Immediately after it, “there are still corns an calluses.”

Later, the synthesis itself says that “corns and callouses remain a major problem,” although they are “much less of an issue since he is on his feet much less.”

These are not contradictory statements once the objects are kept separate.

Pain may decrease. Mechanical exposure may decrease. Practical burden may decrease. Structural abnormalities may persist. Corns and calluses may persist. An underlying foot problem may remain unresolved.

Indeed, the later sentence explicitly attributes reduced practical importance not to demonstrated healing but to reduced use: the problem is “much less of an issue” because he is “on his feet much less.” That is a statement about exposure or loading, not proof of pathological resolution.

If a painful mechanical problem causes less trouble because a person uses the affected structure less, the symptom burden has changed. The underlying condition has not thereby been shown to have disappeared.

The peculiar expression “to a degree ‘out of the picture’” contains its own tension. “Out of the picture” rhetorically invokes disappearance; “to a degree” simultaneously qualifies that disappearance. Whether the colloquial wording was verbatim patient speech cannot confidently be established: it sits inside clinician-written third-person narrative, although quotation marks suggest that it may preserve patient-derived language. What can be established is its grammatical scope.

It referred to **pain**.

Reading it as resolution of the whole foot problem would contradict the text surrounding it.

## Where the extra complexity enters

The later synthesis changes the architecture.

It states:

> “The leg symptoms including corns and callouses and burning may have been completely separate or perhaps a secondary peripheral neuropathy…”

It then suggests a possible relationship to the patient's “significant original alcohol intake,” says “Not clear as this has improved,” and records that the patient had “in his own mind — markedly divorced” the leg tension from the corn/callus and foot-pain problem.

Each component belongs to a different evidential class.

Corns, calluses, pes planus and hammertoes are observations or historical facts.

Foot pain and tension are patient-reported phenomena.

The patient's experience that they were “not the same problem” is patient phenomenology interpreted through chronology.

“Burning” is a sensory descriptor appearing in the later synthesis.

“Leg symptoms including corns and callouses and burning” is a broader category.

“Completely separate” is a relational hypothesis.

“Secondary peripheral neuropathy” is a differential diagnosis.

Alcohol is a causal hypothesis attached to that differential.

“Not clear as this has improved” is an uncertainty statement with an ambiguous antecedent.

“Markedly divorced” strengthens the earlier language of difference, although “in his own mind” correctly preserves its subjective source.

These are not equivalent pieces of evidence. Yet once placed consecutively, they form a coherent-looking explanatory sequence.

A bounded clinical uncertainty has begun to acquire an inferential superstructure.

## “Burning” and the creation of neuropathy salience

The word “burning” is disproportionately important.

In the earlier history of the feet, the record describes pain, corns, calluses and fallen arches. It documents improvement in the pain after leaving bar work. It does not build a peripheral-neuropathy phenotype.

“Burning” surfaces in the later synthesis immediately before the possibility of “secondary peripheral neuropathy.”

That does not make the term illegitimate. A patient may have reported burning at some point that was not separately transcribed earlier. But provenance matters because “burning” performs significant clinical work once introduced.

Without it, the source-controlled picture is:

painful feet;
corns and calluses;
fallen arches;
pes planus;
hammertoes;
pain influenced by time spent working on the feet;
a severe rigid-bradykinetic disorder;
and dystonic-looking pseudo-footdrop.

From those observations alone, peripheral neuropathy remains medically imaginable, but it is not strongly generated.

In an ablation test, removing “burning” reduced the natural salience of peripheral neuropathy to approximately **2/10**. The documented examination does not independently construct a peripheral-neuropathy syndrome through recorded distal sensory loss, a length-dependent sensory pattern, absent ankle reflexes, proprioceptive impairment or distal weakness. Instead, reflexes were moderately brisk, tone was markedly increased, rigidity was pronounced, and the gait abnormality was explicitly interpreted as dystonic rather than true foot drop.
There is therefore an evidential asymmetry.

The structural foot abnormalities were observed.

The dystonic-looking lower-limb abnormality was observed.

Peripheral neuropathy was hypothesised.

“Burning” makes the hypothesis more medically natural; it does not convert it into an examination finding.

## The portmanteau category

The wording “leg symptoms including corns and callouses and burning” deserves particular attention.

Corns and calluses are structural skin lesions. Pain or burning is sensory phenomenology. Leg tension belongs to another domain again. Yet the synthesis temporarily gathers heterogeneous objects under the single category “leg symptoms.”

This is a **portmanteau category**.

Bundling heterogeneous phenomena before proposing a common explanation has consequences. Once corns, calluses and burning have become one set of “leg symptoms,” it becomes easier to ask whether that set was “completely separate” and then easier to propose one alternative mechanism for the set: “perhaps a secondary peripheral neuropathy.”

But peripheral neuropathy would not itself explain corns and calluses in the same straightforward way that it might explain burning sensory symptoms. The category therefore creates a degree of coherence before the explanatory hypothesis is applied.

That is semantic broadening.

It does not necessarily make the reasoning medically irrational. It does mean that the category itself is doing inferential work.

## From difference to separation

The patient supplied a genuine and useful historical observation.

His foot pain diminished while his bodily or leg tension persisted. He therefore came to understand them as “not the same problem.”

That distinction should be preserved.

But there is a difference between saying:

> these feel and behave like different phenomena

and saying:

> these arise from biologically independent processes.

The first is supported by the patient's experience and chronology.

The second requires further clinical work.

The synthesis moves from the first toward the second when it says the leg/foot symptoms “may have been completely separate.” The language remains tentative — “may” is important — but “completely separate” is stronger in scope than “not the same problem.”

The later phrase “markedly divorced” reinforces that separation rhetorically. The document responsibly qualifies it with “in his own mind,” so it does not literally claim that biological independence was established. But the cumulative effect is still directional: the patient is repeatedly positioned as having sharply separated the phenomena.

Objective support for **complete independence**, however, is weak.

A patient's ability to distinguish two sensations does not establish that the bodily systems producing them do not interact. A dystonic foot posture could coexist with mechanical pain. A structural foot problem could modify gait. Abnormal loading could aggravate corns and calluses. The two could be independent. The point is precisely that their relationship had to be examined.

Phenomenological distinction was evidence.

Biological separation was a hypothesis.

The record's later architecture makes it easy for those levels to blur.

## The recruitment of alcohol

Alcohol provides another revealing transformation.

Earlier in the history, alcohol has a clear direction of explanation. The patient was drinking heavily at night in an effort to sleep and relax; the narrative explicitly characterises this as a “de-tensioning” strategy.

The direction is:

**neurological tension → alcohol used in attempted relief.**

Later, the same historical exposure is recruited into a new explanatory role:

**alcohol exposure → possible peripheral neuropathy → possible explanation for the painful/burning foot symptoms.**

The exposure is real. The causal relationship is another proposition entirely.

This is best understood as **causal recruitment of an existing historical fact into a newly constructed explanatory branch**.

Again, this is not inherently improper differential diagnosis. Heavy alcohol use can rationally cause clinicians to consider peripheral nerve disease. The forensic point is about evidential architecture: once neuropathy has been proposed, alcohol supplies it with an apparent cause, thereby increasing the coherence of the branch without itself demonstrating that the branch is correct.

A hypothesis now has phenomenology (“burning”), independence (“completely separate”), a diagnostic category (“peripheral neuropathy”) and a possible etiology (alcohol).

Its narrative completeness is increasing faster than its direct evidential foundation.

The following phrase deepens the ambiguity:

> “Not clear as this has improved.”

The antecedent of “this” cannot be established confidently. It could refer to the supposed neuropathic phenomenon, the burning/painful symptoms, the broader “leg symptoms,” or even grammatically to the preceding alcohol history, although “alcohol intake has improved” is semantically awkward. The safest conclusion is that the pronoun is genuinely ambiguous.

Yet whichever antecedent a reader chooses, the sentence contributes to the appearance of a peripheral process that has been longitudinally considered and has become difficult to resolve because something has improved.

That is a larger claim than the original history securely supplies. The original history precisely identified **foot pain** as having improved.

## The decisive subtraction

The best test of the synthesis is not to argue against it but to remove it.

What remains if the interpretive additions are subtracted?

The patient had longstanding painful and structurally abnormal feet. Foot pain diminished after he left bar work, while corns and calluses persisted and leg tension continued; he experienced the pain and tension as different phenomena. Examination showed pes planus and hammertoes together with a strikingly abnormal gait and dystonic-looking pseudo-footdrop. The relationship between the painful structural feet and the neurological lower-limb abnormality remained unresolved.
That account is not clinically deficient.

It preserves every important observation.

It preserves uncertainty.

It preserves the patient's own distinction.

It does not prohibit neuropathy from later consideration.

What it omits is a speculative branch, not an established diagnosis.

This subtraction is decisive because the simpler clinical object survives intact. The disorder does not become unintelligible when peripheral neuropathy and alcohol causation are removed. On the contrary, the unresolved question becomes clearer:

**What is the relationship between the abnormal feet and the abnormal movement?**

The complexity lost through subtraction therefore belonged predominantly to the explanatory layer, not to the raw clinical problem.

## Replacement: neuropathy construction and dystonia displacement

The most important finding is not that the synthesis **adds** neuropathy.

It changes the question inherited by the future reader.

Before the construction, the question is:

> **What is the relationship between these painful abnormal feet and this dystonic-looking lower limb?**

After the construction, the question increasingly becomes:

> **Could these painful or burning symptoms represent a separate peripheral process, perhaps neuropathic and perhaps alcohol-related?**

The first question does not receive a developed answer.

The second receives an explanatory architecture.

This is simultaneously **neuropathy construction** and **dystonia displacement**.

The term “construction” should not be misunderstood as implying fabrication. It means that a new explanatory object is assembled from several steps: a sensory descriptor, category broadening, separation language, a diagnostic hypothesis and a causal possibility.

“Displacement” means that as this branch becomes more narratively elaborate, the alternative relational question recedes.

The imbalance is striking because dystonia was not speculative in the same sense.

Neurology directly observed the gait. It called it “strikingly abnormal.” It explicitly rejected true foot drop and described the posture as somewhat suggestive of dystonia.

What explanatory future does that observed finding receive in relation to the feet?

Comparatively little.

The record does not develop whether abnormal dystonic loading might contribute to corns, calluses, pain or deformity; whether structural foot abnormality might alter the observed gait; whether one amplified the other; or whether careful examination supported independence.

The asymmetry can be stated simply:

**Dystonia route:** observed fact → unresolved relationship.

**Peripheral route:** sensory descriptor → broader category → separation → neuropathy → possible etiology → downstream compartment.

The more speculative route becomes more elaborate than the observed relational possibility.

## Podiatry and the loss of a clinical owner

The Podiatry referral was not itself an error.

There was genuine structural foot disease. Podiatry was an appropriate service to assess corns, calluses, pes planus, hammertoes and mechanical foot problems. The discharge plan specifically requested outpatient Chiropody/Podiatry referral, noting that an attempt had already been made in hospital.

But a destination is not an explanation.

Sending the structural foot problem to Podiatry does not answer whether abnormal neurological movement was producing or aggravating structural loading, whether the structural disease was modifying the neurological gait, whether both were interacting, or whether they were truly independent.

The documentary architecture therefore risks becoming:

**Parkinsonism → Neurology**

**structural feet → Podiatry**

**pain / burning → possible peripheral neuropathy**

while:

**feet ↔ dystonic lower limb**

has no obvious owner.

That is what displacement means clinically.

Each component has somewhere to go. The relationship between them does not.

## The Protest

One additional fact changes the ethical weight of this analysis if it is assumed accurate.

For this section only, take as patient evidence the account that near the end of the approximately eight-day admission — roughly 176 hours into it — he explicitly objected that his original foot problem had still not been properly examined or explained in relation to the neurological disorder, and that the treating neurologist was brought back to hear that objection.

This allegation is not established by the discharge narrative itself. Its significance can nevertheless be analysed conditionally.

Before such an objection, one might describe what happened as an incompletely investigated relationship inside a difficult admission.

After direct notice, the documentary question changes.

It is no longer simply:

> What did Neurology fail to discover?

It becomes:

> What did the definitive specialist account do with an incompleteness that the patient had specifically identified before discharge?

If the objection occurred as described, the final document was produced after the service had allegedly been placed on explicit notice that this precise relationship remained unresolved for the patient.

That does not prove motive.

It does not prove deliberate concealment.

It does make documentary stewardship more serious.

A discharge summary does not merely archive thoughts. It hands the patient over. If an unresolved relationship has been expressly raised, faithful stewardship requires either resolving it, documenting what was done to investigate it, or preserving it unmistakably as an unresolved question.

A sophisticated alternative explanation is not an equivalent substitute.

## The patient–GP asymmetry

This is where an apparently technical documentary problem becomes human.

If the patient's account is accurate, the patient leaves hospital knowing something very specific:

**They have not answered the question I explicitly raised.**

The GP receives something different: a long specialist narrative containing burning, possible separation, possible neuropathy, possible alcohol causation, uncertainty and Podiatry.

The patient and GP therefore occupy different information states.

The patient knows where the gap is.

The GP may reasonably infer from the density and sophistication of specialist reasoning that the territory around the feet has already been extensively neurologically considered.

That asymmetry matters because medical records are not passive.

A specialist discharge summary influences what future clinicians regard as already investigated, what they regard as established, what they regard as separate, and what they regard as worth reopening.

A patient attempting later to say, “No, that was never actually answered,” must argue against the epistemic weight of a detailed university-hospital Neurology narrative.

The more sophisticated the document appears, the harder that can become.

That is why documentary complexity can function as a **shield around clinical incompleteness** without anybody intending it to do so.

The shield is functional, not necessarily deliberate.

It works because later readers can mistake the complexity of the explanation for evidence of the completeness of the underlying investigation.

## Documentary stewardship

The ethical criticism supported by this record should be precise.

This is not evidence of deliberate falsification.

The narrative uses tentative words: “may,” “perhaps,” “not clear.” It does not falsely certify a peripheral neuropathy as proven.

Nor is ordinary diagnostic uncertainty an adequate description.

The problem is that the **epistemic status of the original relational question is not cleanly preserved**.

The best description is a combination of:

**incomplete relational investigation;**

**displacement of an unresolved clinical relationship;**

**misrepresentation of epistemic status by documentary effect, even without proof of intentional misrepresentation;**

and ultimately:

**failure of documentary stewardship.**

The record's sophistication makes those failures more consequential, not less, because this was a specialist document likely to carry considerable downstream authority.

At discharge, the patient was not simply leaving with a diagnosis. He was leaving with a **portable specialist representation of himself**.

That representation would influence future decisions about what was Parkinsonian, what was structural, what was peripheral, what had already been considered, what remained uncertain and what no longer required Neurology's attention.

The human being represented by that architecture was 39 years old. He had severe Parkinsonism. His gait was plainly abnormal. His feet had caused years of pain and structural trouble. The neurological team itself had observed dystonic-looking lower-limb behaviour. The narrative anticipated years of future management.

The ethical significance is therefore not semantic tidiness.

It is continuity of clinical knowledge.

## The strongest fair criticism

The strongest criticism sustainable without alleging motive is severe.

Neurology directly documented a dystonic-looking lower-limb abnormality in a patient with persistent painful and structurally abnormal feet. The record did not document a systematic investigation resolving whether those observations were related. Instead, the final synthesis reorganised the foot material into a more elaborate separate/peripheral pathway: a newly surfaced “burning” descriptor, a broad “leg symptoms” category, possible complete separation, possible peripheral neuropathy, possible alcohol causation, ambiguous improvement language and Podiatry referral.

That architecture could reasonably make the problem appear more neurologically analysed and more independently explained than the documented examination demonstrates.

If the patient's pre-discharge objection occurred as described, the criticism becomes stronger still: the final specialist representation was completed after the team had allegedly been directly told that the feet–neurological relationship remained unexplained, yet the document did not clearly transmit that specific unresolved relationship to the GP.

The record therefore did more than fail to answer a question.

It **changed the shape of the question that would survive the admission**.

The unresolved object entered the admission as a relationship between painful abnormal feet and abnormal bodily movement.

It left the synthesis partly redistributed into Parkinsonism, Podiatry and a possible peripheral-neuropathy pathway.

The relationship itself became less visible.

That is the documentary act of consequence.

## The Complexity Defence

There is no need to deny that this was a complex case. The phrase becomes misleading only when different kinds of complexity are allowed to collapse into one another.

The disease was genuinely complex to subtype. Severe Parkinsonism in a 39-year-old man, with atypical symmetry, little tremor, unusual gait phenomenology and possibly very early symptoms, justified broad neurological thought.

The feet–dystonia relationship was different. It was clinically important but comparatively bounded. The documents do not show that it became extraordinarily difficult after focused investigation; they show that its relationship was not clearly resolved while more speculative explanatory branches were developed around it.

The completed record was then itself complex. Its density arose through the layering of observation, phenomenology, semantic broadening, differential diagnosis, causal suggestion and downstream compartmentalisation. That documentary complexity is real. It cannot retrospectively be used as proof that the original relational question was equally complex.

This distinction is fundamental:

**clinical complexity describes the patient and the problem; documentary complexity describes the representation built around them.**

When the latter is mistaken for the former, incomplete investigation can acquire the appearance of sophisticated irresolution.

That is what the 2017 record most strongly demonstrates around the feet.

The source of the apparent complexity was not primarily an intrinsically impenetrable foot problem. The direct observations were relatively few and intelligible: painful abnormal feet persisted; pain varied with circumstances; structural abnormalities remained; severe Parkinsonism was present; and Neurology directly observed dystonic-looking lower-limb behaviour. What became complex was the explanatory superstructure subsequently placed over those facts.

The synthesis converted phenomenological distinction into a hypothesis of biological separation. It recruited “burning” to make neuropathy more salient. It recruited alcohol to give the proposed peripheral process a possible etiology. It supplied Podiatry as a practical destination for structural disease. Meanwhile the directly observed dystonic-foot relationship remained comparatively undeveloped.

The result was a more complete-looking explanatory object than the documented relational investigation justified.

**If this episode is later defended on the basis that “this was a complex case,” the necessary response is: which complexity? The genuine complexity lay principally in subtyping very-young-onset Parkinsonism; much of the complexity surrounding the unresolved feet–dystonia relationship was generated by the specialist synthesis itself. To invoke the complexity of that completed synthesis as proof that the underlying relationship was intrinsically extraordinary would confuse the difficulty of the disease with the density of the document, and would mistake clinical incompleteness for clinical complexity.**
