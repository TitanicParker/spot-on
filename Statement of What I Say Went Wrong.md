My case is not that doctors failed to possess hindsight in 2017. It is that they had enough information, early enough, to know that the relationship between my longstanding painful feet and my neurological disease remained unresolved, yet an explanation was allowed to take the place of investigation. That explanation then became embedded in the record, survived repeated reasons to reconsider it, and ultimately formed part of a pathway in which my physical suffering became severe psychological distress. The safeguarding failure is the part I find most serious because, whatever argument remains about diagnosis or causation, the later record itself documents the degree of distress I was experiencing.

The underlying medicine is important but not especially complicated. Idiopathic Parkinson’s disease and PRKN-related Parkinson disease are both Parkinsonian disorders. The important distinction is that “idiopathic” Parkinson’s disease has no identified molecular cause, whereas PRKN disease has one. PRKN disease is also particularly associated with young onset and lower-limb dystonia. Recognised descriptions of PRKN disease note that dystonia, particularly in the legs, can precede the fuller Parkinsonian syndrome and can remain isolated for years.

That matters because the history recorded before Neurology ever constructed its explanation has a strikingly similar shape.

I had experienced painful or “sore” feet from the beginning of my history, around 2004–2005. When my GP encountered me in April 2017, I was attending with bilateral foot pain and an abnormal gait. Over the following weeks the presentation evolved in front of the GP: difficulty initiating movement became more apparent, stiffness and increased tone developed, reflexes were brisk, dexterity deteriorated, work became difficult, and the GP eventually raised the possibility of Parkinsonism and arranged urgent hospital assessment. The original presentation therefore arrived at Neurology as one open feet–gait–tone–movement problem, not as two established conditions called Parkinson’s disease and unrelated foot disease.

Neurology then correctly recognised a severe young-onset Parkinsonian syndrome. I was only 39. The syndrome was relatively symmetrical, with marked rigidity and bradykinesia, brisk reflexes, little tremor and an extremely abnormal gait. Most importantly for the later history, the neurologist himself described the lower-limb appearance as being “somewhat suggestive of a dystonic posture — NOT FOOT DROP.”

The hospital was therefore not looking at a conventional middle-aged presentation of ordinary Parkinson’s disease with an incidental sore foot. It was looking at very-young-onset, atypical Parkinsonism with a dystonic-looking lower limb. The DaTscan request itself referred to the unusual dystonic-looking walk and suspected an autosomal-recessive Parkinsonian disorder. PRKN/PARK2 was within the clinical differential at the time.

What I say went wrong is what happened to the feet after that point.

The record does not show a developed neurological investigation of the relationship between my longstanding painful feet, toe posture, the dystonic-looking lower limb, abnormal gait, loading of the feet and the structural lesions that were visible. Instead, the dystonic observation seems to reach a dead end. In the completed synthesis another explanation appears: the foot or leg symptoms might be “completely separate” or “perhaps a secondary peripheral neuropathy,” with past alcohol use proposed as a possible contributor.

I do not object to a doctor considering peripheral neuropathy. My objection is that a possibility was allowed to occupy the place where investigation should have occurred.

I noticed the underlying problem myself before the admission had completely finished. I did not tell the doctors that I had dystonia. I did not tell them that neuropathy was wrong. I was a patient who had lived with sore feet from the beginning of the illness, and I simply noticed that the feet had received almost no attention. I was drawing attention to the absence of investigation, not proposing an alternative diagnosis.

There was still time to act on that observation. The documentary process remained open after I physically left hospital; the DaTscan was performed and incorporated afterwards before final consultant sign-off. The record was therefore still capable of receiving important information. What I cannot identify is an equivalent attempt to reopen and investigate the unresolved feet–neurology relationship.

Instead, the plan proceeded “as per above.”

For me, that is the point at which an incompletely investigated explanation became a documentary commitment. “As per above” is not wrongdoing in itself. Its importance is functional: what had been written above became the information state from which subsequent care proceeded.

That information state was challenged remarkably quickly.

After neurological treatment with procyclidine and rasagiline I improved dramatically. When procyclidine was later withdrawn for two weeks, the first thing I noticed changing rapidly was the outside-left-foot problem, accompanied by becoming more “shifty” and “unrelaxed.” The symptoms worsened off the neurological medication and improved after treatment was restored.

The important point is not that procyclidine somehow treated a callus. It is that pain arising from the same persistent focal territory at the lateral left forefoot was reproducibly altered by neurological medication: it improved with treatment, worsened when treatment was withdrawn, and improved again when treatment was restored.

That pattern is difficult to reconcile with the painful 5th-MTPJ region behaving as a wholly independent, static foot disorder. A structural lesion may still have been present, but the forces acting upon it — through dystonia, rigidity, abnormal posture, altered gait or another motor mechanism — may themselves have been neurologically determined.

On that interpretation, the callus was potentially both a lesion and a clue: a visible marker of repeated abnormal loading at a site whose pain varied with neurological treatment state. The dechallenge/rechallenge did not establish the precise mechanism, but it provided a concrete clinical reason to investigate the relationship between the neurological disorder, foot biomechanics and the persistent focal lesion, rather than continuing to assume that the neurological and podiatric problems were separate.

The record does not demonstrate that this relationship was investigated.

Then came the independent second opinion.

Professor Daniel Healy did not simply confirm idiopathic Parkinson’s disease. He wrote that I “probably has Park II” and recommended testing PARK2 first, moving to a broader panel if it was negative. By April 2018, my own treating neurologist recorded that PARK2/PRKN was what “we/Prof Healy suspect is the cause.”

That is a critical date for me.

By April 2018 the service knew that I had very-young-onset Parkinsonism; that it was atypical and relatively symmetrical; that the lower limb had looked dystonic; that an autosomal-recessive disorder had been suspected; that an independent specialist thought I probably had PARK2; that PARK2 testing remained available; that my feet continued to cramp; and that the disputed foot territory had already changed with neurological medication state.

They did not need to know my eventual genetic result to recognise that the original separation of the feet from the neurological disease deserved reconsideration.

That is why I describe what followed as path dependence.

The original peripheral explanation did not have to be positively proved again every year. It had already entered the record. Later evidence could therefore be interpreted against an inherited background in which the foot problem had already been allocated elsewhere.

Over the years the boundary was crossed repeatedly. Foot symptoms varied with neurological medication state. Later foot movement was described as Parkinsonian. OFF dystonia became an explicit consideration. Toe curling persisted. The clinical record itself acknowledged that the earliest symptoms had been in the feet. Dopaminergic medication repeatedly altered my experience of the feet.

Yet the peripheral-neuropathy possibility survived.

That is what I mean when I say that the original decision became a mechanism. The decision was not merely one clinical opinion in 2017. Once recorded, it influenced how later evidence was classified, which specialty owned the symptom, what was treated, what was reconsidered and what apparently no longer required fresh investigation.

Meanwhile my suffering became progressively more serious.

By 2023 the record described the problem as a “heavy psychological burden.” I said that walking “psychologically consumes” me and that another walk could feel like a “psychological mountain.” The record also contained continuing bilateral foot difficulty, cramping and consideration of Parkinsonian OFF dystonia.

By 2024 my life was described as “a lot smaller.” Activities had contracted. Foot pain and toe curling were affecting ordinary function.

By 2025 the language recorded in Neurology had become extreme. My feet could be too sore to tie my runners. Mornings were “murder in the feet.” The first 150 footsteps were “torture.” I was trying to put into words how intolerable the experience had become.

The same record also states that when the Parkinson’s medication reached my system, there was relief in the feet.

Yet even there the old formulation remained: “if there is a degree of peripheral neuropathy here.”

I later experienced profound relief from a version of the longstanding foot pain after using Sinemet CR at night. I do not need that response to prove that every historical foot symptom was dystonia. That is not my argument. Its importance is that it supplied another powerful reason to reopen the relationship that had never been adequately investigated in the first place.

Instead, when I formally complained, the historical structure was defended.

The response continued to speak of “peripheral neuropathic pain symptomatologies,” while explaining the Sinemet response as an alteration in the experience of pain “of whatever cause.” It also relied on the fact that I had received an early second opinion from Professor Healy, although Healy’s actual recorded opinion had been that I probably had PARK2, not that idiopathic Parkinson’s disease had been conclusively established.

Eventually the institutional response concluded that there had been no deficit in care.

Since about 2004 I have had a persistent, recognisable point of pain on the outside of my left foot. I can still identify and feel that same place today, and I regard it as part and parcel of the same condition I have lived with throughout this history. No other pain anywhere in my body has persisted for so long in precisely the same location. By late 2017 the record had already shown that this foot territory was neurologically modifiable, because it changed with withdrawal and re-introduction of neurological treatment. Between 2023 and 2025 that same longstanding pain became so severe and psychologically consuming that I was using language about not being able to live with it, although I was describing intolerability rather than suicidal intent. In March 2025, a single overnight dose of Sinemet CR produced profound relief from a version of that same pain. The question for me is therefore not merely whether anyone could have known its precise mechanism in 2017; it is how a pain already shown to be neurologically modifiable, in a patient with young-onset Parkinsonism and suspected PRKN disease, could later become psychologically dangerous without triggering a documented safeguarding response. Given what the treating service already knew, that omission raises one of the most serious questions in the case.

For me, however, the most serious part of this history is not the terminology used for Parkinsonism, the failure to perform PRKN testing earlier, or even the original neuropathy hypothesis.

It is safeguarding.

A clinician can make an uncertain diagnosis. A hypothesis can be wrong. A difficult mixed foot problem can reasonably take time to understand.

But once the medical record itself is documenting that a patient is being psychologically consumed by his physical condition, that his life has become smaller, that walking has become a psychological mountain, that mornings feel like murder and the first footsteps like torture, a new issue exists irrespective of who was right about the original diagnosis.

At that point the question is no longer simply, “What causes the feet?”

It is also:

**“What is happening to the person living inside this unresolved problem, and what are we doing to keep him safe?”**

That is why the safeguarding issue is the greatest issue for me.

I am not saying that the absence of a note proves that absolutely nothing was ever considered or done. I am saying that, in the record available to me, I can see the psychological deterioration being documented in increasingly serious terms, but I cannot identify a corresponding documented safeguarding response proportionate to what was being recorded.

That distinction matters profoundly to me.

The medical dispute could still have been unresolved. The clinician could still have believed that neuropathy remained possible. The genetics could still have been unknown. None of those uncertainties prevented recognition that I was becoming psychologically overwhelmed by prolonged physical suffering.

Safeguarding did not require anybody to concede my theory of the feet.

It did not require an admission that the 2017 discharge synthesis was wrong.

It required recognition of the condition of the patient in front of them.

That is why I regard the later defence of the historical care as secondary to the safeguarding question. Even if every earlier diagnostic decision were assumed, for argument’s sake, to have been reasonable when made, the later record created a fresh and independent question about my safety.

The failure I allege therefore has two connected stages.

The first is **clinical and informational**: a longstanding neurological-foot question was insufficiently investigated, an explanatory possibility filled the gap, and that explanation acquired longitudinal authority despite repeated reasons to reconstruct it.

The second is **human and protective**: while that unresolved pathway continued, the burden on me became severe and psychologically consuming, yet the record available to me does not demonstrate a corresponding safeguarding response.

That second stage is why this is not, for me, simply an argument about retrospective diagnostic accuracy.

I survived the consequences of this history.

My central question is therefore not only why the original explanation was allowed to survive so much contradictory or qualifying evidence.

It is also why, when the consequences for me became visible in the medical record itself, the system appears to have remained primarily concerned with explaining the symptoms rather than safeguarding the person experiencing them.

That is what I say went wrong.
