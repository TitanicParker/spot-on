The Legal Shape of the Case

A second-pass legal-forensic analysis of the Spot On repository read against the canonical clinical record.

Jurisdiction: Ireland · Core period: 2017–2026 · Analytical premise: the pre-discharge Protest occurred, its omission from the travelling record is real, and the Protest created the decision fork described below.

1. Legal Position in One Page

The strongest legal theory is not “failure to diagnose dystonia.” It is that an unresolved, clinically material relationship was expressly identified to Neurology before documentary closure; the service then completed an authoritative synthesis that gave the unresolved foot territory a separate peripheral/structural future without documenting the Protest or demonstrating the relational investigation needed to earn that separation; and the resulting information architecture subsequently governed years of care despite repeated evidence crossing its boundaries.

On the assumed facts, the case has four principal legal dimensions. First is clinical negligence: whether the 2017 and later care fell below the standard of a neurologist of comparable status acting with ordinary care, and whether that breach caused avoidable pain, functional loss or psychiatric/psychological injury. Second is professional regulation: whether the conduct amounted to poor professional performance and, depending on seriousness and knowledge, potentially professional misconduct. Third is institutional governance: whether the hospital and Neurology service created, inherited, maintained and later validated a defective clinical information state. Fourth is data protection and record completeness: not simply whether a historical medical opinion was “wrong,” but whether omission of material contemporaneous dissent and continued unqualified reliance on the resulting record created an incomplete or misleading data state requiring supplementation, restriction or other corrective action.

The phrase “reckless reconstruction” is useful as a factual characterization, not as a standalone Irish tort or offence. Its legal work is to describe the alleged breach: after notice that the core presenting relationship remained unresolved, the service chose a representation that enabled closure and downstream allocation while leaving the observed dystonic relationship underdeveloped. If expert evidence supports that characterization, it can feed negligence, professional-performance, record-integrity and governance findings without any need to prove malice.

2. Assumed Factual Premise

This analysis accepts the following as true because that is the requested legal hypothesis: near the end of the July 2017 admission, after the principal clinical data had substantially been gathered and after Parkinson's disease had already begun to be acted upon administratively, the patient expressly protested that the bilateral foot problem that had brought him into the system remained unresolved. The Protest created a real choice: reopen and study the relationship, preserve it explicitly as unresolved neurological work, or proceed to closure for stated reasons. The Protest did not enter the authoritative travelling discharge account. The later synthesis instead provided a peripheral/structural destination for the unresolved territory.

That premise is intentionally narrower than an allegation of malice. It does not assume anyone wanted to harm the patient. It assumes notice, omission, a decision fork, and a post-notice synthesis.

3. What the Clinical Record Adds to the Repository

3.1 The GP did not send “Parkinson's plus unrelated feet”

The GP referral described an unusual festinant and stiff gait in a patient attending with bilateral foot problems; worsening initiation of movement; marked stiffness and increased tone in upper and lower limbs; brisk reflexes; possible cogwheeling; inability to relax; toe clawing in bed; worsening dexterity; and deteriorating employment function. Parkinsonism was posed as one possible underlying diagnosis, not as a settled organising category. This is an integrated pre-specialist clinical object. Canonical record: DOCUMENT 02, CLIN-000847–CLIN-000884.

3.2 Neurology itself observed the key competing relationship

The 2017 examination recorded markedly increased tone, strikingly abnormal gait, a footdrop-like appearance “somewhat suggestive of a dystonic posture — NOT FOOT DROP,” later summarised as the left lower limb kicking out “like a foot drop,” together with pes planus and hammertoes. The same specialist record diagnosed young-onset Parkinsonism and specifically entertained PRKN/PARK2. Canonical record: DOCUMENT 04, CLIN-001085–CLIN-001136 and CLIN-001520–CLIN-001525.

3.3 The 13-year history has a disease-coherent candidate

The neurologist wrote that symptoms may have begun approximately thirteen years earlier and, in the DaTscan indication, described “some form of symptoms for upto 13 years” followed by progression into a more typical Parkinsonian picture; the walk was “almost dystonically unusual,” and recessive Parkinsonism was suspected. A contemporaneous clinician-to-clinician referral stated that the patient “seems to have had symptoms of Parkinson's for the last 13 years.” Canonical record: CLIN-000946–CLIN-000963; CLIN-001394–CLIN-001402; CLIN-001724–CLIN-001729.

The later molecular report does not rewrite 2017, but it provides an unusually specific retrospective stress test: PARK2/PRKN is characterised by bradykinesia, rigidity and lower-limb dystonia, with lower-limb dystonia sometimes remaining isolated for years, slow progression and good L-DOPA response. The later external specialist concluded that the patient's Parkinsonism and dystonia were due to compound heterozygous Parkin mutations. Canonical record: genetic report CLIN-006220–CLIN-006239; St Vincent's conclusion CLIN-003226–CLIN-003229.

3.4 The separation branch remained less evidentially secure than its operational role

The 2017 synthesis described “leg symptoms including corns and callouses and burning” as possibly “completely separate” or “perhaps a secondary peripheral neuropathy,” possibly related to prior alcohol intake, while saying it was “Not clear as this has improved” and that the patient had “in his own mind — markedly divorced” leg tension from corns/calluses and foot pain. Yet later treating records still used conditional language such as “If there is a degree of peripheral neuropathy here.” The repository's central distinction therefore survives the primary record: neuropathy functioned as an authorised explanatory bucket without ever becoming comparably secure.

3.5 The body repeatedly crossed the partition

In 2018, withdrawal of procyclidine was followed by rapid return/change of the outside-left-foot phenomenon together with worsening tension. In later years Neurology characterised a foot-throw/footdrop-like phenomenon as Parkinsonian in the presence of normal strength; OFF dystonia was considered; the Parkinson's nurse assessed leg pain and shuffling as OFF-related; severe pain persisted despite high-dose pregabalin; and Sinemet was acknowledged to help morning pain before the eventual profound response to Sinemet CR. Canonical record includes CLIN-001914–CLIN-001920; later Neurology records and 2025 complaint response.

3.6 The harm was not merely theoretical

By 2023–25 the foot problem was described as the major problem, a “heavy psychological burden,” psychologically consuming, limiting repeated walks, and later as “murder in the feet” with the first steps described as torture. The clinician's 2025 response acknowledged profound relief from Sinemet CR and expressed that he could only begin to imagine what the patient had gone through in pain. These facts matter because damages in negligence are not awarded for diagnostic untidiness; they are awarded for injury caused by breach.

4. The Protest as the Legal Fork

The repository correctly identifies the Protest as the hinge, but the clinical record makes its legal significance sharper. Before the Protest, the issue can still be characterised as incomplete specialist reasoning. After the Protest, on the assumed facts, there is actual notice of incompleteness.

The legally important transition

Before: Neurology may have failed to appreciate or complete a relationship. After: the patient expressly points to the unresolved relationship while the patient, phenotype and document remain available for further work. From that point, proceeding without reopening or accurately preserving the unresolved state becomes a choice capable of being judged as a professional act.

The fork matters in negligence because reasonable care is assessed in the circumstances actually confronting the practitioner. It matters in professional regulation because a serious omission after direct notice can be qualitatively different from an unnoticed mistake. It matters in record integrity because the authoritative document is not merely a spontaneous snapshot: it is completed after the service has been told that the account is insufficient in a clinically material place.

The DaTscan timing strengthens the documentary point without proving motive. Physical discharge did not freeze the record. The still-open document was capable of accepting later diagnostic information, and the abnormal DaTscan was incorporated. Therefore a defence that the opportunity for documentary correction had simply ended is difficult to sustain on this premise.

5. “Reckless Reconstruction”: What the Phrase Can Properly Mean

“Reckless reconstruction” should not be pleaded as though it were an independent cause of action. It is a concise description of the alleged factual mechanism:

  1. An integrated feet–gait–tone problem enters Neurology.
  2. Neurology recognises real Parkinsonism and directly observes a dystonic-looking lower-limb sign.
  3. The relationship between that sign and the painful feet is not demonstrated as having been adequately investigated.
  4. The patient expressly identifies that incompleteness before documentary closure.
  5. The final synthesis does not carry the Protest forward as an unresolved warning.
  6. Instead it constructs a medically respectable peripheral/structural route: burning → possible complete separation → possible neuropathy → alcohol plausibility → Podiatry → “Not clear.”
  7. The route is sufficiently authoritative to govern future care even though the neuropathy proposition remains qualified.
  8. Later contradictory neurological information is accommodated locally rather than triggering reconstruction of the founding relationship.

The word reckless is strongest if used in its ordinary forensic sense: a serious disregard of the risk that an authoritative representation was materially incomplete and would shape future care. It should not be confused with any technical criminal-law test unless a lawyer deliberately pleads such a test on appropriate evidence.

What makes the allegation severe is not that the prose was sophisticated. It is that the sophistication of the final account may have exceeded the depth of the underlying relational investigation. The legal question is whether ordinary specialist care permitted a clinician, after direct notice of incompleteness, to give an unresolved relationship a separate explanatory future without the examination, testing, explicit preservation of uncertainty or follow-up ownership that would justify that move.

6. Clinical Negligence

6.1 Governing standard

Irish clinical negligence remains governed by the Dunne v National Maternity Hospital principles. The central standard is whether the practitioner failed in a way that no practitioner of equal specialist or general status and skill, acting with ordinary care, would have failed. A general or approved practice does not necessarily protect a defendant if that practice has inherent defects that should be obvious on proper consideration. The Supreme Court in Morrissey v HSE reaffirmed that the standard is that of a comparable professional acting with ordinary care and that expert evidence will ordinarily establish the applicable professional standard.

6.2 The best breach formulation

Not: “The neurologist should have diagnosed PRKN in July 2017.”

Better: “A reasonably careful neurologist faced with severe very-young-onset Parkinsonism, a directly observed dystonic-looking lower limb, painful abnormal feet, an integrated GP referral, and an express pre-discharge objection that the feet remained unexplained would not have closed and transmitted the case as a peripheral/structural residual without adequately investigating, documenting or preserving the unresolved neurological relationship and arranging an appropriate route for its reassessment.”

This formulation avoids hindsight. It relies on contemporaneous facts already in the record. PRKN later strengthens relevance, but it is not needed to create the 2017 duty of inquiry.

6.3 A continuing breach theory

A second negligence theory concerns failure to reassess. It should not be pleaded generically. It should identify the specific later events that put pressure on the original model: treatment-state dependence in the foot territory, normal strength despite pseudo-footdrop, Parkinsonian reclassification, OFF-dystonia reasoning, OFF-related pain/shuffling, incomplete response to maximal pregabalin, and dopaminergic pain response. A court would still need expert evidence about when the standard of care required global model review rather than local treatment adjustment.

6.4 Causation is the hard legal bridge

Even a serious breach does not establish liability without causation. The case must show, on the balance of probabilities, that proper handling would probably have led to materially different care and less injury. The strongest causation questions are therefore practical: would a proper 2017 relational assessment or an explicit “unresolved dystonia–foot relationship” flag likely have changed treatment, testing, specialist referral, medication strategy or follow-up? At what later point would reasonable reassessment probably have led to dopaminergic treatment of the relevant pain state? How much of the subsequent pain and psychological burden would probably have been avoided?

The fact that Sinemet CR later produced profound relief is powerful but not self-proving. The defence can argue that later response does not prove the historical mechanism or that treatment would appropriately have been delayed because of dyskinesia and impulse-control risks in young-onset disease. Expert movement-disorders evidence is therefore essential.

7. Professional Regulation: Poor Professional Performance and Misconduct

Section 57 of the Medical Practitioners Act 2007 permits complaints on grounds including professional misconduct and poor professional performance. The Act defines poor professional performance as failure to meet the standards of competence, whether in knowledge, skill or their application, reasonably expected of practitioners practising that kind of medicine.

Irish appellate authority emphasises that poor professional performance requires a serious falling short. Professional misconduct can also exist without fraud, dishonesty or moral turpitude where professional conduct seriously falls below expected standards.

7.1 Why poor professional performance is a natural fit

If the assumed facts are proved, the allegation is fundamentally about application of specialist knowledge: observing dystonia but not developing its relationship to the presenting foot problem; allowing a speculative peripheral branch to acquire operational force; failing to preserve a directly raised unresolved issue; and failing over time to reconstruct the model when later evidence repeatedly crossed it. That is exactly the kind of knowledge/skill/application inquiry for which “poor professional performance” exists.

7.2 When the case could cross into professional misconduct

The misconduct threshold would become more plausible if evidence showed that the clinician appreciated the specific material risk of misleading future care and nevertheless authored or maintained a representation that concealed the unresolved status. It does not require proof of a wish to harm. But the more the allegation depends on conscious record manipulation rather than seriously deficient clinical judgment, the more precise the proof of knowledge and authorship chronology must be.

The key point is that the case need not be inflated into dishonesty to be grave. A serious professional failure can be disciplinary even where the clinician sincerely believed the chosen model was defensible.

8. Record Integrity and Misrepresentation of Epistemic Status

The repository's strongest original contribution is the idea of misrepresentation of epistemic status. Legally, this should be translated carefully.

A medical record can be literally accurate about individual sentences and still be materially misleading in what it communicates about the state of knowledge. “Perhaps neuropathy” is not false merely because neuropathy is unproved. “Not clear” is not false merely because investigation is incomplete. The potential wrong lies in the combination: if a future reader is reasonably caused to understand that Neurology adequately worked through the relationship and reached a considered residual uncertainty, when in truth the relationship was expressly identified as unfinished and not adequately reopened, the record carries more closure than the clinical process earned.

On the assumed facts, omission of the Protest is therefore not merely missing patient commentary. It removes the contemporaneous warning that the adequacy of closure itself had been challenged. That matters because the discharge synthesis was expressly designed to consolidate discussions, explain decisions and travel to the GP/patient.

Legal translation: the central record allegation is not “the doctor wrote an incorrect diagnosis.” It is “the doctor/hospital created and circulated an authoritative representation whose structure materially understated unresolved clinical uncertainty after direct notice, thereby changing downstream decision-making.”

This theory can support negligence and professional-regulation claims. It may also matter to data-protection completeness. It should not automatically be labelled “falsification” unless evidence proves knowing falsity.

9. Hospital and Departmental Responsibility

9.1 Vicarious and concurrent liability

Under Irish civil liability law, multiple actors may be concurrent wrongdoers, including through vicarious liability, breach of joint duty or successive acts causing the same damage. A hospital/HSE defendant may therefore face liability for acts of employees/agents as well as any independently pleaded organisational negligence. The precise defendant structure requires solicitor review of employment, indemnity and service arrangements.

9.2 Direct institutional negligence

The institutional theory is strongest where it identifies system duties rather than treating “the department” as a person capable of subjective guilt. Potential failures include:

  • lack of ownership for the unresolved Neurology–Podiatry interface;
  • failure to ensure a direct pre-discharge patient objection was documented and clinically dispositioned;
  • failure to trigger movement-disorders or multidisciplinary reassessment when neurological and mechanical foot evidence repeatedly converged;
  • continuity systems that allowed a provisional peripheral hypothesis to become stable infrastructure without an exit condition;
  • governance processes that later assessed treatment reasonableness without adequately testing the integrity of the founding information state.

9.3 Capability is not participation, but it matters to foreseeability and standard

The repository appropriately distinguishes departmental capability from proof that particular experts treated the patient. The legal use of the Galway Parkin/dystonic-foot expertise should therefore be cautious. It does not prove negligence merely because colleagues had published relevant work. It can, however, undermine any suggestion that the clinical relationship was conceptually unknowable in 2017 and may inform expert evidence about the professional environment and available referral/escalation options.

10. Complaint Handling and Governance

The later QPS history creates a separate governance question. HSE guidance distinguishes purely clinical-judgment complaints from mixed complaints. Importantly, HSE's own clinical guidance gives an example where the medical element is clinical judgment but allegations of feeling unheard or that concerns were not taken seriously are non-clinical elements capable of being investigated through Your Service Your Say.

That matters here because, if the Protest is assumed true, the later complaint was not solely: “the neurologist chose the wrong medical hypothesis.” It also concerned whether an express objection to incompleteness was omitted, whether the authoritative record accurately represented the state of the case, and whether later review relied on that challenged record. Those are governance and record-handling issues as well as clinical judgment.

The 2025 QPS letter recorded the complaint as concerning bias/anchoring and prolonged preventable pain but routed it as relating solely to clinical judgment. The later SIMT outcome found no deficit in care, relying materially on the reasonableness of levodopa timing and the existence of alternative diagnoses. The legal/governance question is therefore whether the review answered the actual mechanism alleged: post-notice reconstruction, omission of the warning, downstream propagation and failure to rebuild the relationship.

A governance finding of “no deficit in care” does not necessarily answer whether the record was complete, whether the complaint pathway properly separated clinical and non-clinical issues, or whether later institutional reliance was safe once the underlying information architecture itself was under challenge.

11. Data Protection and the Continuing Medical Record

This part requires discipline. The Data Protection Commission expressly warns that an old diagnosis or medical opinion does not become “inaccurate personal data” merely because a later diagnosis differs. A historical opinion may remain accurate as a record of what a clinician believed at the time. The stronger GDPR theory here is therefore completeness, context, restriction and continuing use, not erasure of historical opinion.

11.1 Article 16: completeness

Article 16 covers inaccurate data and also incomplete data, including completion by supplementary statement. If the Protest occurred and was material to understanding the state of the discharge decision, a serious question arises whether the operative record is incomplete if it carries the explanation for closure but not the contemporaneous challenge that closure was incomplete. The remedy may be supplementation/annotation rather than rewriting the historic document.

11.2 Article 18: restriction while accuracy is contested

The DPC states that where a data subject contests accuracy, restriction can apply while the controller determines accuracy. The factual record should therefore be examined closely for what was requested, what restriction was acknowledged (if any), what processing nevertheless occurred, and whether any applicable exception was invoked.

11.3 Articles 19 and 24; Article 82 / section 117

If rectification, completion or restriction was required, questions may arise about notification to recipients and whether the controller had adequate organisational measures. Separate from DPC enforcement, Article 82 GDPR and section 117 of the Data Protection Act 2018 permit a private data-protection action for material or non-material damage caused by an infringement. This route is not a substitute for proving clinical negligence, and the DPC cannot decide whether a medical diagnosis was clinically correct.

Most defensible data-protection formulation

The issue is not “delete the 2017 opinion because later genetics changed the diagnosis.” It is whether the hospital's continuing data state and governance use remained accurate and complete for their purposes once contemporaneous dissent, later neurological evidence and later genetic clarification were known, and whether the controller properly handled requests to supplement or restrict that data while the dispute was live.

13. Likely Defences — and the Real Answer to Each

“Neuropathy was only a possibility.”

That defeats an allegation of falsely declaring definitive neuropathy. It does not answer whether a provisional hypothesis acquired disproportionate organisational force or whether it displaced an observed neurological relationship after notice.

“Structural foot disease was real.”

Agreed. The case should never deny it. The allegation is mixed-mechanism failure: structural pathology can coexist with neurologically abnormal posture/loading and still require Podiatry.

“Parkinsonism was correctly diagnosed.”

Agreed, and later genetics strengthens that. The allegation concerns what happened to the dystonic lower-limb relationship inside a real Parkinsonian/PRKN disease process.

“Levodopa was reasonably delayed because of young age and dyskinesia risk.”

This is a serious clinical defence. The answer is not that levodopa had to be started immediately in July 2017. The question is whether the unresolved foot/dystonia relationship required better investigation, preservation and later testing, and whether later evidence eventually made continued avoidance or compartmentalisation unreasonable.

“Many reviews and investigations occurred.”

Activity is not the same as global model revision. The repository's strongest systems argument is constrained corrigibility: substantial local care can coexist with failure to revisit the organising partition.

“The patient received copies and could disagree.”

Patient access to a record does not by itself equal acceptance of its clinical architecture. On the assumed facts, the patient had already objected before closure. The issue is whether the specialist record faithfully preserved that unresolved state for future care.

“Later genetics is hindsight.”

Correct if used to say clinicians should have known the genotype. Incorrect if used to block all retrospective evaluation. Later genetics is legitimately used as a biological stress test of which 2017 findings were central versus peripheral, while the 2017 standard of care must still be judged on contemporaneous knowledge.

14. Limitation and Procedural Urgency

Important

Any civil clinical-negligence claim requires immediate Irish legal advice on limitation. Personal-injury limitation periods are generally short, and Irish law commonly applies a two-year period by reference to accrual/date of knowledge. The precise “date of knowledge,” effect of continuing treatment, later discovery, multiple wrongs, disability (if any), and the procedural requirements for clinical-negligence proceedings are fact-sensitive and should not be assumed from this report.

The 2025 treatment response, complaint chronology and 2026 genetic confirmation may be highly relevant to knowledge, but they do not automatically reset time for every earlier wrong. A solicitor should map each alleged breach and damage period separately.

15. The Proof Architecture

If this were being prepared for a solicitor, counsel, expert, regulator or independent investigator, the evidence should be organised around a small number of propositions rather than the full rhetorical density of the repository.

  1. Input: prove the integrated GP referral and pre-specialist problem state.
  2. Observation: prove the 2017 dystonic-looking lower-limb finding, structural feet and young-onset Parkinsonism/PRKN differential.
  3. Notice: under the assumed premise, establish the Protest by witness evidence, contemporaneous traces and disclosure where available.
  4. Fork: show the record remained open and that alternatives to closure existed.
  5. Output: analyse the exact post-notice wording: separation, burning, neuropathy, alcohol, “Not clear,” “markedly divorced,” Podiatry.
  6. Provenance: obtain native drafts, dictation timestamps, audit logs, version history, source notes and any trainee/ward communications capable of dating the wording.
  7. Propagation: trace where the 2017 categories were inherited downstream.
  8. Contradiction: isolate independent later events that crossed the partition rather than descendants of the original hypothesis.
  9. Causation: obtain movement-disorders expert evidence on what reasonably should have happened at each material point and what difference it probably would have made.
  10. Damage: document pain, function, medication burden and any psychiatric injury with independent clinical evidence.
  11. Governance: compare the actual complaint with what QPS/SIMT investigated and the information state they relied upon.
  12. Data: map every Article 16/18/19 request, response, processing event and recipient.

15.1 The single most important disclosure question

When, by whom and from what source did the consequential July 2017 phrase cluster first enter the document? The repository is correct that this is the most direct route from documentary function toward subjective intent. If the language demonstrably existed before the Protest from identifiable bedside material, the post-Protest reconstruction allegation narrows. If it appeared or materially changed after the Protest and solved the exact closure problem the patient had raised, the legal seriousness rises sharply.

16. What This Analysis Does Not Need — and What It Does Not Prove

The legal case does not need proof that every corn, callus or episode of foot pain was dystonia. It does not need proof that peripheral neuropathy was impossible. It does not need proof that PRKN should have been molecularly diagnosed in July 2017. It does not need proof that delaying levodopa in every young patient is negligent.

On the current evidential record, it also does not establish fraud, conspiracy, deliberate falsification, corruption, perverting the course of justice, or an intention to injure. Those are materially different allegations requiring proof of dishonest or criminal states of mind.

The severe but supportable allegation is narrower:

After direct notice that a clinically material relationship remained unresolved, specialist authority may have converted unfinished neurological work into an authoritative separate/peripheral management state, omitted the contemporaneous warning that closure was disputed, allowed that state to govern longitudinal care despite repeated contradictory signals, and later participated in or was protected by institutional processes that did not squarely reconstruct the founding decision.

If expert evidence confirms that no reasonably careful neurologist would have handled the case that way, the facts can support negligence. If the conduct represents a serious falling short of professional competence, it can support poor professional performance. If the record-handling and conscious disregard of the unresolved status are sufficiently serious, professional misconduct becomes a live regulatory question. If hospital systems and governance contributed, institutional liability and governance accountability follow their own routes. If the continuing data state was incomplete or improperly processed after formal challenge, data-protection remedies may arise separately.

17. Conclusion

Read with the clinical record, the three-document repository is strongest when it stops trying to prove motive and instead identifies the legal event with precision. The event is not the initial recognition of Parkinsonism. That diagnosis was substantially real. The event is not the mere existence of an alternative neuropathy hypothesis. That hypothesis was medically possible. The event is the post-notice reorganisation of an unresolved clinical relationship into a durable information structure.

On the assumed facts, the patient told Neurology that the original foot problem remained unanswered at exactly the point when the service still had the patient, the observed dystonic sign, the young-onset Parkinsonian syndrome and an unfinished discharge account. The service then produced a sophisticated document whose reasoning about genetics and Parkinsonism was far more developed than the documented study of the painful dystonic foot relationship. The unresolved territory was given destinations—Podiatry, possible neuropathy, alcohol-linked plausibility—rather than preserved as an unresolved neurological join.

That distinction is legally consequential because medical records are instruments of future care. The resulting synthesis could be relied on by people who never saw the bedside work or the Protest. Over the following years, the patient's body repeatedly produced information inconsistent with a clean partition, yet the system was able to update locally without reconstructing globally. The later PRKN result does not prove negligence retrospectively. It demonstrates that the 2017 dystonic lower-limb finding belonged centrally to the disease that was already under consideration, while the peripheral-neuropathy branch remained conditional.

The case therefore has the shape of a potential clinical negligence claim coupled with a serious professional-performance allegation, an institutional information-governance failure, and a fact-sensitive data-protection dispute. “Reckless reconstruction” is an apt description of the alleged mechanism if used carefully: not to assert criminal recklessness, but to describe conscious closure after notice through a representation that carried a foreseeable risk of misleading downstream care.

The most important next legal question is not “Can the patient prove malice?” It is: Can independent neurological expert evidence say that, given what was known and expressly raised at the time, no reasonably careful comparable neurologist would have allowed the feet–dystonia relationship to be closed, represented and then longitudinally carried in this way? If the answer is yes, the legal case becomes concrete.
