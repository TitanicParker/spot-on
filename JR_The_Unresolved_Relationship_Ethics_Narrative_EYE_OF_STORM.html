A clinical narrative about uncertainty, records and specialist authority

The Unresolved Relationship

A man entered specialist Neurology with painful abnormal feet, abnormal gait and pathological movement. Neurology correctly recognised Parkinsonism and itself observed a dystonic-looking lower limb. In a university department with demonstrable contemporary expertise in dystonia, young-onset parkinsonism and Parkin disease, the patient says he objected before discharge that the feet—the problem that had started the journey—still had not been explained. The decisive challenge arrived after Parkinson’s disease had already been acted upon administratively, but before the clinical and documentary account was complete. What followed is a story not principally about whether Parkinsonism existed, but about what a specialist record did with the relationship that remained unresolved.

Chronology: 2017–2026 · Central object: painful feet + abnormal gait + abnormal tone + dystonic-looking left lower limb + very-young-onset Parkinsonism

I. The Problem That Entered Neurology

April 2017

The story began as one clinical problem. The patient transferred to a new GP with bilateral foot pain and a walking problem. Over the following weeks the GP watched him deteriorate: his gait became increasingly abnormal, his muscles appeared unusually tense and stiff, his dexterity worsened, and he began to look Parkinsonian. His functioning deteriorated far enough to put his employment at risk. The GP therefore referred him urgently to Neurology.

At that stage, there were not two separate problems called “Parkinsonism” and “feet.” There was one developing sequence: painful feet first, followed by an increasingly obvious disorder of gait, tone and movement.

Where we are Spring 2017 · GP referral · the clinical object is still integrated: feet, gait, tone and movement belong to one unfolding story.

The patient was then admitted to Neurology for eight days. During that admission, the department became confident that he had a Parkinsonian syndrome. At the same time, the feet were recorded as having pes planus, hammertoes and mild pitting oedema, and the gait was strikingly abnormal. Most importantly, the left lower limb had a foot-drop-like appearance that Neurology itself described as “somewhat suggestive of a dystonic posture — NOT FOOT DROP.”

By the time the admission was nearing its end, Neurology therefore had two important facts in the same patient: a convincing Parkinsonian disorder and an objectively abnormal left lower limb behaving in a dystonic-looking way, attached to structurally abnormal painful feet.

Observed neurological sign: “somewhat suggestive of a dystonic posture — NOT FOOT DROP.”

The relationship among those findings had not yet been explained. The record had described the feet. It had described the abnormal gait. It had described the left lower limb as dystonic-looking rather than weak. What it had not done was say what that neurological phenomenon might have to do with the painful feet attached to it.

The real-world departmental setting

This was not an expertise-poor environment. Timothy Counihan, then associated with Galway, had already co-authored a 2013 JAMA Neurology study of Parkin disease in which young-onset parkinsonism, dystonia and long disease courses were central clinical features. One of the reported Parkin cases began with focal dystonia of the left foot years before Parkinson’s disease was recognised. Four years later, in August 2017, four authors from the Department of Neurology, University Hospital Galway / National University of Ireland Galway published a Neurology Clinical Reasoning paper titled “A demure teenager and her dystonic foot.” That case began with an abnormal gait and a foot-drop appearance caused by dystonic plantar flexion rather than weakness; later parkinsonian signs led to genetic testing that confirmed compound-heterozygous parkin/PRKN disease, and treatment included procyclidine.

The publication does not prove that its authors examined this patient, contributed to his July 2017 care, or that the treating neurologist had access to the manuscript before publication. Its evidential value is narrower and important: the department and its immediate academic environment demonstrably possessed the conceptual capacity to recognise the very combination at issue—dystonic foot phenomenology, apparent foot drop without weakness, young-onset parkinsonism and Parkin disease.

II. The Problem Still There at Discharge

July 2017 · hour 176 of 192

Roughly 92% of the way through the admission, the patient brought the original problem back into focus. He pointed out that the bilateral foot problem that had started his medical journey had still not been accounted for. In a simple sense, that observation was correct: the record had described the feet and had described the dystonic-looking left lower limb, but it had not explained the relationship between them.

This was not a new complaint emerging at the end of admission. It was the patient returning Neurology to the problem the GP had sent in the first place.

The Protest: the temporal hinge

Before it: there is an unresolved clinical relationship.

After it, if the patient’s account is corroborated: there is notice that the originating foot problem remains unresolved, followed by a decision about whether to reopen it or proceed.

That distinction matters because direct notice changes the ethical character of what follows. Once the service had been told that the original foot problem remained unanswered, closure was no longer merely the passive continuation of uncertainty. Alternatives remained available: reopen the relationship, re-examine, seek movement-disorders input, retain ownership, document disagreement, arrange a defined review, or proceed while explicitly recording why.

What is still unanswered What is the dystonic-looking left lower limb doing to the painful foot attached to it?

The eye of the storm: after commitment, before completion

There is one fact that changes the atmosphere around the Protest. By the time the patient says the feet remain unresolved, the case is no longer sitting entirely inside a neutral diagnostic conversation. Parkinson’s disease has already begun to leave the building.

The surviving discharge synthesis states that the patient had signed Long Term Illness Scheme forms and that “we have posted these for him.” The same surviving artefact contains a 9 July results-at-transcription-finalisation entry. PCRS later confirmed that the treating consultant’s certified LTI application was submitted on 9 July 2017, before the DaTscan. PCRS also stated that LTI applications can be submitted in advance of confirming diagnosis and before DaTscan findings.

This does not make the LTI application improper, does not make the Parkinsonian syndrome false, and does not prove why the feet were not reopened. Its significance is temporal and structural: an outward institutional commitment had already been made while the clinical and documentary process was still unfinished.

The Protest therefore arrived after commitment but before completion. Earlier in the admission, returning to the feet would simply have meant continuing an open investigation. At this point, reopening remained clinically possible, but it would occur inside a case whose principal neurological identity had already been acted upon outside the immediate diagnostic encounter.

The patient was not asking Neurology to abandon Parkinsonism. He was asking a narrower question: what does the neurological disorder you have identified have to do with the painful feet that brought me here? Reopening might still have produced a mixed answer, a largely separate answer, or continuing uncertainty. But it would have required the service to preserve visibly that the case remained incomplete in the precise place the patient had identified.

Capability, participation and bedside return

The real-world departmental capability matters here because the issue was not whether medicine in 2017 possessed the concepts required to understand such a phenotype. The Galway-authored August 2017 Neurology paper classified an apparent foot drop as dystonia when weakness was absent, followed emerging parkinsonian signs to compound-heterozygous parkin/PRKN, and discussed dystonia as a feature that can distinguish Parkin-related disease in young patients. The older 2013 Parkin paper, with Counihan among its authors, shows that Parkin disease and dystonia were already within the academic landscape before this admission.

The fair inference is therefore about capacity, not unproved personal participation. The department could reason at exactly this level. The unresolved question is whether that capability was returned to this patient’s feet–gait relationship after the Protest, or whether the expertise mainly entered the theoretical synthesis. The mechanism need not be sprawling: institutional commitment was already in motion; the patient exposed one unresolved seam; the department possessed the intellectual tools to understand its significance; and the final record could either reopen that seam or allocate it elsewhere.

The decision now in view The Protest did not occur before commitment. It occurred after Parkinson’s disease had been administratively committed, while the patient, phenotype and discharge document were still available for correction.

III. How the Completed Record Organised It

The completed discharge account is therefore the answer we can actually inspect to that post-Protest decision environment. It did not answer the notice by returning to the feet and developing the relationship between the painful structural abnormalities and the dystonic-looking left lower limb. Instead, it created a different explanatory route.

The earlier history of corns and callouses became “corns and callouses and burning.” The patient’s distinction between foot pain and leg tension was recast as the problems being “markedly divorced” in his own mind, within a formulation that now referred to leg pain. Alcohol was drawn into the explanation, and the painful foot/leg symptoms were described as “perhaps a secondary peripheral neuropathy”, with the relationship left “Not clear as this has improved.”

Nearby, the observed neurological sign—“dystonic posture — NOT FOOT DROP”—did not receive an equivalent relational account explaining what it might mean for the painful abnormal feet.

The discharge synthesis then became more theoretically sophisticated. Because this was a very young patient with convincing Parkinsonism, Neurology widened the differential beyond ordinary idiopathic Parkinson’s disease and specifically considered PRKN/PARK2 and other recessive forms of Parkinsonism. In the same reasoning, PARK9 was introduced as “the neuropathy question.”

This created an asymmetry. The possible neuropathy now had an explicit place in the genetic theory. The already observed dystonic-looking left lower limb still had no developed relationship to the painful feet.

The emerging contradiction

Neurology had seen a dystonic-looking left lower limb in a very young patient with convincing Parkinsonism while seriously considering PRKN/PARK2. Yet the same dystonic finding that could support the PRKN phenotype was not used to reopen the unresolved foot problem. The painful feet remained on a separate neuropathy path.

The contradiction could be deferred because Neurology did not immediately test the PRKN hypothesis genetically. The discharge reasoning itself said that “one of the chief difficulties with genetic testing is whether or not it would change the treatment plan.” Levodopa was also being deliberately withheld in this very young patient. The formal management instruction was simply: “Plan/Recommendations: As per above.”

The practical result was that the dystonic-looking lower limb, the separate neuropathy explanation for the painful feet, and the possible PRKN phenotype could remain alongside one another without any test or detailed neurological study of the feet forcing their relationship to be decided.

13 July 2017

After the patient had physically left hospital, but while the discharge document was still being completed, the DaTscan was performed. It was abnormal, as Neurology had suspected. The radiologist reported appearances compatible with Lewy body disease or idiopathic Parkinson’s disease; the neurologist commented that this was hardly Lewy body disease. The result was incorporated into the still-open discharge synthesis before later final sign-off.

One part of the case therefore received new objective confirmation after discharge: the Parkinsonian disorder. The unresolved relationship between the painful feet and the dystonic-looking left lower limb did not receive an equivalent post-discharge reassessment before the completed account travelled forward. The document was demonstrably corrigible enough to absorb information that strengthened the already-committed Parkinsonian account; the surviving artefact does not show an equivalent reopening of the information that destabilised the disposition of the feet.

What has changed The Parkinsonian side is objectively stronger. The feet–dystonia relationship remains where the discharge architecture had already placed it.

For the GP, this distinction was largely invisible. The GP had not witnessed the admission, the bedside reasoning or the Protest. The GP depended on the specialist synthesis to know what had been established, what remained provisional and what still required ownership. A detailed discharge account assigning destinations to Parkinsonism, Podiatry and possible neuropathy could reasonably look like the completed product of specialist consideration.

IV. The Record Begins Meeting the Patient Again

August–September 2017

Only after that discharge architecture had been completed did the treatment course begin to test the patient in real life. Procyclidine was titrated through the GP from August 2017. The response was striking: by September he was doing well, and the later record described dramatic improvement on procyclidine and rasagiline.

Prof Daniel Healy then reviewed him and wrote that he “probably has Park II”, recommending that PRKN/PARK2 be tested first. For the patient and GP, this looked like strong confirmation that Neurology had understood the case: treatment was working, and an external expert independently favoured the same monogenic Parkinsonian direction. But that confirmation arrived after the discharge record had already separated the painful feet from the dystonic/Parkinsonian frame.

Later, when procyclidine was withdrawn, the treating neurologist recorded that “the first thing he noticed changing and changing rapidly was the return on the foot, of the outside left corn.” At the same time the patient became more “shifty” and “unrelaxed”, with worsening tension and movement, and improved again after restarting treatment.

This does not require the proposition that procyclidine literally created or removed a corn. The narrower point is that a focal outside-left-foot phenomenon changed rapidly with neurological treatment state in the same patient whose left lower limb had already been described as dystonic-looking. The later evidence was beginning to join, in the body, what the discharge record had kept apart.

September 2017–March 2019

Podiatry then supplied something Neurology had not: a detailed anatomical record of what was actually happening in the feet. It repeatedly documented clawed toes, callus, offloading requirements, toe props, insoles, tight gastrocnemius/soleus and recurrent pressure pathology around the left fifth metatarsal region.

When the patient was re-referred in 2019, the referral itself said that his foot problems related to “increased tone and abnormal gait relating to Parkinson’s disease” and described “clawing feet” and pain. On that same podiatry assessment, the recorded risk status included “No neuropathy.”

Podiatry therefore did not merely identify independent structural feet. It documented the recurrent mechanical territory—especially the left lateral forefoot—while explicitly recognising abnormal neurological tone and gait as relevant to those foot problems.

Restore the original object Painful feet + abnormal gait + abnormal tone + dystonic-looking left lower limb + very-young-onset Parkinsonism.

The left foot now becomes an anatomical tracer through the record. Neurology knew that the outside-left-foot state changed with neurological treatment. Podiatry knew that the recurrent mechanical lesion sat in the left fifth-MTPJ/lateral forefoot region, and later explicitly related the foot problems to increased tone and abnormal gait while recording “No neuropathy.”

Yet the painful-foot problem continued to travel inside the explanatory route created in 2017: burning → leg pain → possible peripheral neuropathy → “the neuropathy question.” The important point is not that every corn or callus was neurological. It is that the same territory being carried forward under the neuropathy explanation was simultaneously generating evidence of abnormal loading, neurological treatment sensitivity, and Parkinsonian tone/gait influence.

V. Evidence Crossing the Partition

2020

Neurology itself then went further. The recurrent left-sided gait phenomenon was again described as looking somewhat like foot drop, but this time the neurologist explicitly wrote that it was “actually parkinsonian” because dorsiflexion, plantarflexion, inversion and eversion power were all normal. The same examination recorded exquisite sensitivity of the feet, excessive foot movement and an urge to move them.

The record now contained a striking continuity: in 2017 the left lower limb had looked like foot drop but was judged dystonic rather than weak; after treatment withdrawal the outside-left-foot problem changed rapidly with neurological state; Podiatry repeatedly found focal left-lateral mechanical pathology; and Neurology itself was now saying that the apparent left foot-drop phenomenon was Parkinsonian. Yet this remained a local observation rather than triggering reconstruction of the longstanding painful-foot problem.

September 2021–2022

Levodopa was introduced in September 2021 while procyclidine/Kemadrin was being withdrawn because of raised intraocular pressure. By 2022, even while the patient was otherwise functioning well, the feet still had a distinctly state-dependent character: “Most mornings the feet take 20–25 mins to warm up,” with the corns and calluses producing a “coronal pain” around the edges. The patient himself wondered whether it was neuralgic. He was also adjusting Sinemet according to anticipated physical demands.

The foot problem therefore persisted into the levodopa era not as a static piece of podiatric history, but as something varying with time, activity and neurological treatment state.

2023

By 2023 the old partition was no longer being tested by scattered observations. The foot problem had become a major clinical crisis. In March, the Movement Disorders service recorded “? neuropathic pain” in both feet and legs while the patient was already taking 600 mg/day of pregabalin, yet he was reporting debilitating pain and “no quality of life.”

In July, the neurologist explicitly considered whether recurrent left-lower-limb cramping represented Parkinsonian dystonia and judged an OFF dystonia more likely if it was dystonic. In the same consultation he acknowledged that “the very first symptoms were in the feet all those years ago” and pursued Fabry testing as a possible neuropathy–Parkinsonism overlap.

By September, the Parkinson’s nurse was more direct still: with severe foot pain, shuffling and reduced mobility, she assessed that “the leg pain and shuffling is off related.”

What has changed The record now contains neuropathic labelling, explicit OFF-dystonia reasoning and an OFF-related pain/shuffling assessment in the same year.

The result was not resolution but coexistence. Neurological treatment-state explanations were now explicit within the record, while the original neuropathy allocation still remained in circulation.

VI. The Explanations Begin to Separate Pharmacologically

2024

Pregabalin remained at 150 mg four times daily. Neurology recorded that it had removed much of the foot pain—especially on the right—but left-sided pain remained, while the toe curling during standing and darts did not respond to pregabalin at all. The Fabry investigation pursued through the neuropathy–Parkinsonism overlap route was also negative.

Treatment was now separating components of the phenotype experimentally. A drug directed at neuropathic pain could reduce part of the pain burden without touching the abnormal toe posture, while the earlier procyclidine withdrawal had produced rapid change in the outside-left-foot phenomenon alongside deterioration in movement and tension.

The record was accumulating pharmacological information about which parts of the foot problem moved with which neurological systems, but still without reconstructing the original feet–dystonia relationship.

Pharmacological dissociation

Neuropathic treatment helped some pain but did not alter the abnormal posture. Other neurological treatment had previously changed the left-foot state itself. The foot phenotype was no longer behaving as one undifferentiated neuropathic object.

VII. 2025: The Contradiction Becomes Unavoidable

March 2025

The foot pain had again become overwhelming. The record described the first approximately 150 steps as “torture” and acknowledged that once Sinemet “hits his system” the feet feel better from a pain perspective. Yet in the same clinical account the old explanation remained available: “If there is a degree of peripheral neuropathy here, the alcohol being gone will hopefully slow the deterioration of that.”

The proposed response was Sinemet CR. When the patient subsequently took it, the later account recorded profound relief. The treating neurologist later acknowledged that Sinemet had been known for some time to help the ongoing pain.

Eight years after the dystonic-looking left lower limb was first observed, the record now contained a concrete treatment-state relationship between dopaminergic therapy and disabling foot pain, while neuropathy still appeared in conditional language: “if there is a degree.”

Evidential weight by 2025 Dopaminergic responsiveness of the foot pain is concrete and repeatedly observed; peripheral neuropathy remains conditional rather than established.

When the patient finally challenged the historical explanation, the response did not abandon the neuropathy pathway. Instead, the architecture became more elastic. The neurologist accepted that Sinemet had long been known to improve the pain, but argued that this did not identify the pain generator because Sinemet could lessen the experience of pain “of whatever cause.”

At the same time, peripheral neuropathy was defended not as a diagnosis but as a reasonable possibility that had never been intended to exclude Parkinson’s disease. Evidence for neurological influence could therefore be accepted without requiring neuropathy to disappear, while the fact that neuropathy had never been proved could itself be answered by saying it had only ever been provisional.

By this stage, the frame could absorb almost any new observation without requiring reconstruction of the original feet–dystonia relationship.

VIII. The Architecture Defends Itself

July 2025

The mature architecture becomes visible in the neurologist’s own defence to the Medical Council. He summarised the patient’s complaint as alleging that everything had been framed through “an unproven neuropathy” and alcohol, and rejected that account by pointing to the fact that Parkinson’s had always remained central, that levodopa had eventually been used, and that a beneficial pain response had been documented.

Yet when he returned specifically to neuropathy, he did not identify evidence that had ever established it. He said instead that there had been a “risk” or “worry” of peripheral neuropathy, cited the later Fabry investigation as evidence that the issue had been considered, and expressly stated that neuropathy had not been definitively diagnosed, only that it remained a “reasonable concern.”

The defence therefore reproduced the same structure the chronology had created: Parkinsonism could be fully real and actively treated; levodopa could improve the pain; neuropathy could remain unproved; and none of those facts required the original neuropathy branch to lose legitimacy.

More importantly, later events generated within that branch—such as Fabry testing—could themselves be presented retrospectively as evidence that the branch had appropriately remained live. The longitudinal descendants of the classification could now help defend the architecture that had given those descendants their clinical meaning in the first place.

Asymmetric falsifiability

Neurological evidence could be accepted without displacing neuropathy. Neuropathy could remain unproved without losing its place.

IX. The Biological Endpoint

2026

Independent genetic testing then identified two pathogenic PRKN/Parkin variants in compound heterozygosity. The later specialist interpretation attributed both the Parkinsonism and the dystonia to the Parkin mutations.

That does not prove that every corn, callus or episode of foot pain was dystonic. Nor does it rewrite what clinicians were entitled to know in 2017. It settles something narrower and more important for understanding the record: the dystonic component was not an incidental abnormality sitting outside the principal disease. It belonged inside the same PRKN disease process that Neurology had already been considering in 2017.

The later genetics therefore reverses the documentary hierarchy. The observed dystonic-looking lower limb, which received little explanatory development in relation to the feet, is now known to have been biologically central. The neuropathy pathway that carried the painful feet for eight years never progressed beyond an uncertain and conditional proposition.

X. What the Record Actually Did

Seen from the end of the story, the 2017 partition accomplished something specific. It converted an unresolved relationship into a completed management architecture. Parkinsonism became the central neurological diagnosis. The structural feet could be sent to Podiatry. The painful or sensory component could travel under possible peripheral neuropathy. The relationship between those compartments could remain “Not clear.”

That meant the case could move forward without the original feet–gait–tone relationship ever having to be resolved. For clinicians who later inherited the record, “Not clear” no longer necessarily looked like unfinished work. It arrived inside an authoritative specialist synthesis and could therefore be treated as the result of specialist consideration.

Possible neuropathy became the forward-moving carriage for the unresolved relationship. New observations were repeatedly added inside that architecture rather than forcing it to be rebuilt. The record became richer, but the basic partition remained recognisable.

From administrative commitment to foreclosure

The LTI submission should not itself be called foreclosure. Its importance is that it establishes prior institutional commitment: by 9 July, Parkinson’s disease had already been acted upon administratively while the diagnostic process and discharge synthesis remained unfinished. The Protest then arrived inside that changed decision environment.

Administrative foreclosure emerged later in the sequence: commitment was already in place; the patient directly challenged the unresolved relationship; the completed specialist synthesis nevertheless allowed the existing Parkinsonian commitment to remain coherent by giving the feet a separate explanatory future; and downstream systems could then rely on the resulting information state without reopening the clinical work beneath it.

administrative commitment Protest / notice no visible relational reopening documentary completion downstream reliance

The mechanism is therefore compact rather than sprawling. The LTI did not force the decision. It changed the environment in which the decision was made. The department’s own theoretical capability could then make the completed synthesis highly sophisticated without requiring the unresolved feet–dystonia relationship to be physically re-investigated. In that sense, the same expertise that made the record persuasive also helped make the resulting information state durable.

Earned uncertainty versus unfinished work

The phrase “Not clear” could mean two very different things to downstream readers. It could mean: Neurology investigated this relationship properly and, despite appropriate specialist work, the answer remained uncertain. Or it could mean: the relationship was never adequately worked through, but the case was nevertheless organised into a management plan and discharged.

Those are not ethically equivalent states. Once the Protest disappeared from the travelling account and the final synthesis moved forward, the GP had no reliable way to distinguish them. Specialist authority supplied the missing implication: if Neurology had written “Not clear” inside a detailed discharge account, a reasonable GP could understand that as earned uncertainty, not unfinished work.

The record therefore transferred more than uncertainty. It transferred the appearance that the uncertainty had already been professionally processed. That altered the burden of action. Neurology no longer had to demonstrate why the feet–dystonia relationship could safely remain unresolved; the patient or some later clinician would instead have to produce enough new evidence to justify reopening what the specialist record already appeared to have considered.

Unearned documentary uncertainty

Unfinished work can acquire the appearance of uncertainty reached after sufficient specialist inquiry. Once that happens, the burden of reopening moves downstream.

The patient and GP move into different information states

The patient could leave Neurology incompletely diagnosed in relation to the problem that had actually brought him there: Parkinsonism had been recognised, but the relationship between the painful abnormal feet and the dystonic neurological phenomenon remained unresolved.

The GP, meanwhile, could receive an account that was incompletely informative in exactly the corresponding place. Because the specialist synthesis supplied destinations for each component—Parkinsonism, Podiatry, possible neuropathy—and expressed the remaining relationship as specialist-authored “Not clear,” the GP had little reason to understand that this uncertainty might represent an unfinished relational investigation rather than the considered endpoint of one.

The same act could therefore operate in two directions at once: the patient was left without a complete explanation, while the GP was given a sufficiently complete-looking explanation not to know what was missing.

Complex patient, or complex documentary architecture?

The originating clinical object was relatively simple to describe: painful abnormal feet, abnormal gait and tone, and a dystonic-looking left lower limb in the same very young patient with a Parkinsonian syndrome. What became elaborate was the documentary explanation that allowed those elements to be distributed into different futures.

The peripheral route received sensory vocabulary, causal separation, alcohol as a candidate cause, a named neuropathy hypothesis, uncertainty framed as improvement, patient-attributed separation language, and eventually a genetic branch. The observed dystonic-looking lower limb received no equivalent relational account of what it might be doing to the painful feet.

This is asymmetric explanatory infrastructure. The question is not whether neuropathy was medically conceivable. It is why one unproved possibility received a developed future while an observed neurological relationship did not.

Because the critical neuropathy construction was assembled contiguously in the final specialist product, the complexity itself became clinically persuasive. A GP or later reviewer could reasonably infer that the apparent complexity reflected the patient, rather than recognising that much of the complexity lay in the architecture used to organise an unresolved relationship.

Endogenous validation

A record can create a category. The category can shape later treatment and investigation. Those treatments and investigations create more records. Years later, the accumulated records can make the original category look independently confirmed.

That mechanism is endogenous validation. Later date does not necessarily mean independent evidence.

In this case, the neuropathy pathway acquired exactly that kind of longitudinal footprint: terminology, pregabalin treatment, Fabry investigation and repeated conditional references. Yet it never became an established diagnosis, while increasingly direct neurological-foot observations accumulated alongside it. Pregabalin use, neuropathy terminology and Fabry testing therefore cannot simply be counted as three independent confirmations of the original neuropathy proposition without examining their provenance.

Why the Protest and the completed synthesis belong to the same mechanism

If the Protest occurred, one part of the information state disappeared: the GP was not told that the patient had directly challenged the adequacy of the foot disposition before discharge. At the same time, another part became more elaborate: the final account supplied Parkinsonism, monogenic reasoning, Podiatry, possible neuropathy, alcohol and “Not clear” as a coherent explanation of where the pieces belonged. The surviving discharge artefact remained open long enough to incorporate later diagnostic information, including the DaTscan, yet it preserved the clinicians’ interpretation of the feet without preserving the alleged contest of that interpretation. The destabilising fact that closure had been challenged was absent, while the stabilising explanation for closure was present.

XI. The Ethics of the Case

The case is therefore not best described as an ordinary allegation that clinicians were simply wrong. Medicine is allowed to be uncertain. The ethical question concerns what specialist authority is permitted to do with uncertainty when the specialist record will possess more authority over the patient’s future than the patient’s own account of what remains unresolved.

Misrepresentation of epistemic status

The central concern is that an unresolved relationship concerning the patient’s originating problem was converted into an authoritative, portable account that could reasonably make later clinicians believe the relationship had been adequately worked through when it had not. The patient depended on the specialist service not only to diagnose him, but to represent faithfully what had been established, what had merely been hypothesised, and what still required ownership.

If his pre-discharge Protest that the feet remained unresolved was then absent from the account that travelled forward, the record also removed the contemporaneous warning that apparent closure had been disputed. That is an epistemic warning-label removal: the later reader sees the explanatory product without seeing the fact that its adequacy had been challenged at the time.

Relational stewardship

Specialist documentation does not merely store facts. It allocates what future clinicians should regard as settled, provisional or still open. Later clinicians reasonably rely on the division of professional labour and therefore inherit not merely observations but the specialist’s allocation of what still deserves doubt.

That is why the ethical harm is informational as well as clinical. The record can alter the future patient–GP relationship by changing what the GP believes still needs investigation. The patient continues to experience the unresolved feet; the GP continues to work from an authoritative account that appears to have already situated them.

Institutional capability and responsibility

The ethical question is not confined to what one neurologist personally knew. The 2017 record itself shows departmental and senior input into the case, while the contemporary academic record shows that the Galway Neurology environment possessed relevant expertise in Parkin disease, dystonia and young-onset parkinsonism. That does not prove that every expert participated in this admission. It does establish why the distinction between capability, participation and bedside application matters. A department can possess the right knowledge, contribute that knowledge to a sophisticated synthesis, and still fail to return it to the unresolved bodily relationship at the moment when doing so could alter the case.

This makes the institutional duty narrower and more concrete: when a patient exposes an unresolved seam in a case already being theoretically enriched by the department, somebody must own the return loop—observe → formulate → consult expertise → return to the patient → test the improved formulation → revise. Expertise that remains only in the document can strengthen closure without improving the examination that closure purports to summarise.

Continuity and the duty of global model review

The same admitting neurologist remained the patient’s treating neurologist across the following eight years and later described the relationship as extending from the “very first inpatient evaluation” through annual reviews. If the Protest is corroborated as having been put directly to him before discharge, then founding notice and later longitudinal management sit within the same professional continuity.

That continuity does not prove deliberate concealment. It does not. Its narrower significance is that simple handover loss becomes a less complete explanation for persistence. The clinician who knew that the patient believed the feet had not been adequately resolved was also the clinician under whose care the later neuropathy language, treatment choices, OFF phenomena, pain responses and eventual complaint accumulated.

Over time, the outside-left-foot change with procyclidine withdrawal, recurrent left-lateral mechanical pathology, the foot throw described as Parkinsonian, explicit OFF-dystonia reasoning, the Parkinson’s nurse’s OFF-related pain/shuffling assessment, and clear Sinemet responsiveness repeatedly crossed the original partition.

At some point, the ethical duty was no longer merely to treat each manifestation locally. It was to ask whether the founding partition itself had become untenable. That is the threshold for global model review: when later evidence repeatedly crosses an original diagnostic partition, continuity of care requires more than local relabelling or treatment adjustment. It requires asking whether the original causal separation should itself be redrawn and whether downstream clinicians need an updated synthesis.

Constrained corrigibility

The care was not static. Symptoms were reconsidered. Medications changed. Dystonia was entertained. OFF phenomena were recognised. Fabry testing was ordered. Levodopa responsiveness was acknowledged. Local facts could move.

But those changes largely occurred inside the inherited architecture. The founding causal map was not comparably reopened. The system therefore had high local degrees of freedom with low global degrees of freedom: it could look responsive because it continually adapted at symptom level while remaining resistant to reconsidering the organising model that determined what those symptoms were allowed to mean.

This is constrained corrigibility. The allegation is not that nothing was ever done. It is almost the opposite:

A great deal could happen inside the frame without the frame itself being reconstructed.

XII. The Irreducible Question

The entire chronology can now collapse back into its simplest form.

GP sends one integrated problem Neurology identifies Parkinsonism and a dystonic-looking left lower limb Parkinson’s is administratively committed patient says the feet remain unresolved final synthesis preserves the commitment while separately allocating the feet the separation travels for eight years patient-specific evidence repeatedly crosses it later PRKN biology joins Parkinsonism and dystonia the founding unresolved relationship becomes visible again

The strongest ethical formulation is therefore not: they got the diagnosis wrong. It is that specialist and institutional authority may have transformed unfinished clinical work into portable apparent knowledge, thereby changing what the patient, GP and later clinicians were able to know remained unresolved. The eye of the storm is small: commitment came first; the Protest exposed the incompleteness; completion followed without a visible relational reopening.

If the Protest is corroborated, its importance lies in exactly that transformation. Before it, there was an unresolved clinical relationship. After it, there was notice that the relationship remained unresolved and therefore a decision about whether to reopen it or proceed. If the travelling record preserved the reasoning that supported closure while omitting the contemporaneous fact that closure had been challenged, later readers were not given the information needed to reconstruct the decision that had actually been made.

The patient then carried the unresolved problem in his body while the record carried a different allocation forward. Over time, he had to produce increasingly strong evidence—treatment-state changes, anatomical recurrence, OFF phenomena, dopaminergic pain response and ultimately genetic clarification—to recover a question he says he had already raised before discharge.

Medicine is entitled to uncertainty. What specialist authority is not entitled to do is make unfinished uncertainty look like completed knowledge and then require the patient to spend years proving that the unanswered question was unanswered all along.
