03 · Ethics narrative

The Unresolved Relationship

The human and institutional meaning of a correction signal that did not travel with the record, and the two continuous histories that followed.

Ethical synthesis, not an evidential source. Facts remain subordinate to the clinical record and underlying originals.
Contents

I. Before the record knew the answer

There was a period in 2017 when the case had not yet been organised into the account that later clinicians would inherit. During that period the patient's painful feet, abnormal gait, toe clawing, increased tone, movement-initiation difficulty and functional decline belonged to one open problem. Structural foot disease was real, but the neurological meaning of the gait and posture was also developing.

Neurology then recognised severe Parkinsonism and directly observed a lower-limb posture that looked dystonic rather than weak foot drop.

At that moment the patient was not yet living inside a settled story. The patient, the signs and the explanations were still capable of changing one another.

II. The phase change

The discharge synthesis changed that relationship.

A specialist record is not merely memory. It is an instrument through which future clinicians encounter the past. It selects, organises and transmits.

In this case the Parkinsonian side acquired a rich neurological and genetic future. The foot side acquired structural, peripheral and podiatric routes. Those routes may all have been medically plausible. The ethical question is whether their eventual documentary authority exceeded the relational inquiry that visibly supported them.

The patient moves from being the subject of an open inquiry to being the subject of an authoritative explanatory record.

After that point, new clinicians do not begin where the GP began. They begin with the summary.

III. The Protest

For this personal reconstruction, the pre-discharge Protest is taken as fixed. Its ethical significance does not depend on the patient knowing the diagnosis. He did not have to say “this is dystonia” or “this is PRKN disease.” He only had to identify a mismatch between the work completed and the problem that still mattered.

A patient is uniquely positioned to know certain things: continuity, pain, function, response to treatment and whether the problem he sought help for feels answered.

The Protest was therefore a correction signal.

The ethical question is not whether clinicians were obliged to agree with it. It is whether the signal retained enough epistemic force to reopen, qualify or explicitly preserve uncertainty before closure.

IV. Two continuous histories

Once that signal failed to travel in the authoritative account, two histories could diverge.

The institutional history could proceed as though the relevant allocation had been sufficiently resolved.

The patient's history could proceed from the knowledge that he had already said the opposite.

Neither later attendance nor medication adherence requires him to forget that event.

The record may forget a correction signal. The patient is not thereby obliged to forget that he gave it.

A 2023 “misalignment” or a 2025 complaint may therefore be the institutional reappearance of an old disagreement, not the birth of a new one.

V. The patient's epistemic status

The patient can be used by a clinical model in two very different ways. He can be a source capable of changing the model, or he can become material the model is allowed to interpret.

The 2017 synthesis preserves statements attributed to the patient that support separation. On the Protest premise, his contrary challenge does not travel with equivalent force.

credible when confirmatory; corrigible when contradictory.

The ethical concern is not that clinicians must accept the patient's causal theory. They need not. It is that the patient's evidence must retain some capacity to change the inquiry when lived reality does not fit the model.

VI. Later care and the paradox of adaptability

The later Neurology record is not one of simple indifference. Medications changed. Symptoms were revisited. Foot-drop-like movement was reclassified. OFF dystonia was considered. Pain and shuffling were treated as OFF-related. Neuropathic medication was adjusted. Metabolic testing was pursued. Levodopa was introduced. Later dopaminergic benefit was accepted.

This is real professional activity.

The paradox is that a system can be highly adaptive locally while remaining comparatively unreconstructive about the historical meaning of the founding decision.

What evidence would have been allowed to make the original allocation lose?

VII. The levodopa asymmetry

The discharge synthesis treats levodopa with unusual prospective care. Risks are explained. Long-term strategy is recorded. Patient understanding is recorded. Possible future regret is anticipated.

That is responsible documentation.

But it also creates a striking contrast. The record is much less transparent about the clinical work by which the founding foot problem and dystonic-looking lower-limb phenomenon were separated after direct notice.

Years later, when the patient complains, the well-documented question is easier to answer: why was levodopa delayed?

The poorly documented question is harder: what exactly happened after the patient said the originating problem remained unresolved?

The ethical concern is not that the levodopa documentation was improper. It is that documentary asymmetry can determine which later question becomes easiest for an institution to hear.

VIII. Question migration

A complaint can change shape as it passes through systems.

The patient's upstream question is about completion and provenance. The clinician's response can become a defence of longitudinal management. Governance can ask whether care was reasonable within the accumulated record. Data governance can ask whether the record accurately reflects the opinion held at the time. Later governance can ask whether new diagnosis requires reopening a completed process.

Every question may be valid, yet the original one can remain unanswered.

Do not allow an easier downstream question to stand in for a harder upstream one simply because the record is better equipped to answer it.

IX. The originating clinician's special position

A later clinician can only know what was transmitted. The originating clinician may know more because he was present.

If a material pre-closure event was omitted from the record, the clinician who participated in that event cannot ethically occupy the same position as a later reader who never knew it happened.

He may sincerely continue to hold the same medical opinion. But when explaining or defending the history, he must account for both what the documentary record contains and what he personally knows occurred outside it.

Otherwise the absence of documentation can begin to erase information from the account of a person who actually possessed that information.

X. External correction

The eventual external result is ethically subtle. It does not show that the original clinicians were generally incompetent. Their suspicion of PRKN/PARK2 was perceptive. It also does not establish that every foot symptom was dystonic.

What it does show is that the ultimate biological architecture included both Parkinsonism and dystonia. PRKN disease can also include lower-limb dystonia that precedes fuller Parkinsonism for years.

That makes the 2017 dystonic-looking lower-limb observation more important without converting it into hindsight proof.

Why did the neurological relationship that was already visible receive less relational development than the peripheral branch that remained conditional?

XI. Governance and historical memory

An institution needs finality. Not every later medical development should reopen every historical review.

But finality is ethically different from immunity from relevant evidence.

If later evidence bears directly on the phenomenon that was under dispute at the founding moment, an institution should at least be able to explain why that evidence does or does not change the epistemic status of the old decision.

Historical records do not need to be erased. They can remain accurate historical objects while being annotated, qualified or understood differently.

The deeper ethical question is not whether the past can be rewritten. It is whether the institution has a route by which the past can be re-understood.

XII. The strongest innocent explanation

The patient had a difficult, mixed presentation. Structural foot disease was obvious. Severe Parkinsonism was real. A peripheral component was plausible. The patient's own descriptions supported some distinction. The neurologist conducted extensive work, sought advice, considered genetics and cautiously planned treatment. The Protest may have been sincerely understood as a remaining podiatric concern rather than as a challenge to the neurological formulation. Later treatment response can be explained by mixed mechanisms.

Nothing in the documentary architecture alone disproves that account.

XIII. The strongest concerning explanation

The competing possibility is that the service knew before closure that the original foot problem remained materially unresolved, but the remaining opportunity was used more effectively to complete a defensible explanatory record than to expose the disputed relationship to discriminating inquiry.

The resulting record then became the information state later care inherited. Repeated boundary-crossing evidence was handled locally. The patient's old disagreement reappeared only intermittently. The mature record was later deployed in defence of the pathway, and governance validated the pathway through that accumulated record.

This explanation is more serious. It also requires more proof.

XIV. What fairness requires

Fairness does not require the institution to accept the patient's causal interpretation. It requires something more basic:

  • preserve the difference between observation and hypothesis;
  • preserve material disagreement;
  • distinguish independent evidence from inherited repetition;
  • allow later contradictory facts to change the status of old conclusions where warranted;
  • and do not make the patient's failure to be recorded into evidence that he failed to speak.

The patient's account remains one evidential source. It is not automatically controlling. But it must be allowed to remain a continuous source, not restarted whenever the record begins listening again.

XV. The ethical finding

The deepest issue is corrigibility.

A functioning clinical system must preserve a route by which material contrary evidence can change an existing decision.

The danger is not sophistication. Sophistication is valuable. The danger is explanatory power without a corresponding obligation to encounter evidence capable of defeating the explanation.

Expertise should reduce uncertainty about reality, not merely reduce the vulnerability of a decision to contrary reality.

The case is therefore best understood as a question about what happened when an open clinical problem crossed a documentary threshold and became an authoritative history.
