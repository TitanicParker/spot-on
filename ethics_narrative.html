03 · Ethics narrative

The Unresolved Relationship

One continuous clinical history, and the ethical consequences when a material correction signal does not travel with the record that later governs care.

Ethical synthesis for understanding the case. It is not an evidential source. Facts remain subordinate to the clinical record and underlying originals.
Contents

I. Before the record knew the answer

There was a period in 2017 when the case had not yet been organised into the account later clinicians would inherit. Painful and structurally abnormal feet, abnormal gait, toe clawing, increased tone, movement-initiation difficulty and functional decline belonged to one open problem. Neurology then recognised severe Parkinsonism and also directly observed a lower-limb posture that looked dystonic rather than weak foot drop.

At that point the patient, the signs and the explanations were still capable of changing one another.

II. The Protest

For this reconstruction, the pre-discharge Protest is fixed. Its significance does not depend on the patient already knowing the diagnosis. He did not have to say “this is dystonia” or “this is PRKN disease.” He identified something more basic: the problem that brought him into care had not been adequately addressed and discharge was imminent.

The Protest was therefore a correction signal before closure.

The ethical question is not whether the clinician had to agree with the patient. It is what happened to corrective information after it was received while meaningful options remained.

III. The phase change

The discharge synthesis changed the kind of object the case was. A specialist record is not merely memory. It selects, organises and transmits.

The Parkinsonian side acquired a rich neurological and genetic future. The foot side acquired structural, peripheral and podiatric routes. Those routes may all have been medically plausible. The question is whether their documentary authority became greater than the relational inquiry visibly supporting them.

After that point later clinicians did not encounter only the patient. They encountered the patient through a specialist-authored information state.

IV. One history, documentary overlay

There are not two histories.

The Protest happened within the same history as the admission, the discharge decision, the later treatment, the later distress, the complaint and the eventual external reopening. The patient remembers that he made it because it is part of his life. The originating clinician participated in it because it is part of the same clinical encounter.

What changed was representation. The travelling record did not preserve the Protest's place or force. Professional interaction could therefore proceed through a documentary overlay that made the episode look more settled than the patient knew it to have been.

A patient cannot be required to adopt the omissions of his medical record as omissions from his own life.

When the patient later identifies the omission and places it formally before the clinician and institution, he is not introducing a new history. He is restoring an omitted part of the existing history to the decision-makers' attention.

V. The patient's epistemic status

The patient is not a diagnostic authority, but he is an evidential source with privileged access to continuity, pain, function, treatment response and what he actually said.

The 2017 synthesis preserves statements attributed to the patient that support separation. On the Protest premise, his contrary challenge does not travel with equivalent force.

The ethical concern is therefore not that every patient statement must be accepted. It is whether contrary patient evidence is allowed to remain visible and capable of changing the inquiry rather than being useful only when confirmatory.

VI. The originating clinician's special position

A later doctor may know only what the chart contains. The originating clinician is differently situated because he personally participated in the omitted pre-closure event.

This does not establish that he remembers it years later. It means that when the historical pathway is later explained or defended, the inquiry cannot simply collapse personal participation into documentary silence.

How should an originating clinician explain a resolution by reference to a documentary account that does not preserve a material event in which he personally participated?

That question concerns provenance and professional explanation before it concerns dishonesty.

VII. Later care: help within the model versus help that can change the model

The later Neurology record is not one of simple indifference. Medications changed. Symptoms were revisited. Foot-drop-like movement was reclassified. OFF dystonia was considered. Pain was treated. Levodopa was introduced. Later dopaminergic benefit was accepted.

This is real care.

The harder ethical distinction is between helping within an established model and helping in a way that permits the established model itself to lose authority.

A clinician may sincerely wish to relieve suffering while still operating within a historical allocation that the patient challenged before it was finalised. That is a structural tension; it does not require a conscious wish to protect an old decision.

VIII. Boundary-crossing evidence

Several later events put pressure on the founding feet–movement boundary: procyclidine withdrawal and return of the foot phenomenon, later neurological reclassification of a foot-drop-like movement, morning warm-up, OFF-related pain and shuffling, persistent toe curling, dopaminergic pain relief, and eventually external recognition of dystonia with PRKN/PARK2 confirmation.

None proves that every historical foot symptom was neurological. Their ethical significance is corrigibility:

What evidence was capable of making the original allocation lose?

IX. Psychological distress belongs downstream unless evidence places it earlier

The record later documents severe psychological distress. That distress should not be projected backwards to explain away the original presentation or the pre-discharge Protest if it was not a presenting feature.

Nor should its cause be assumed. The fair longitudinal question is whether living for years with an unresolved problem, while care proceeded through a representation that did not preserve the original objection, formed part of the context in which distress developed.

X. The levodopa asymmetry

The discharge synthesis treats levodopa with unusual prospective care. Risks, strategy, patient understanding and possible future regret are all documented.

That can be responsible documentation. Its significance is comparative: the record is much less transparent about the clinical work by which the original foot problem was separated from a directly observed dystonic-looking lower-limb phenomenon after notice.

Years later, “why was levodopa delayed?” is therefore easier to answer from the record than “what exactly happened after the patient said the originating problem remained unresolved?”

A successful defence of levodopa timing does not answer the prior question of documentary closure.

XI. Professional pretence: functional before intentional

“Professional pretence” should be used carefully.

At the functional level it means professional interaction proceeds as though the documentary representation exhausts the relevant history. That can be observed without proving anyone lied.

The stronger form—knowing pretence—would mean an actor consciously proceeds as though an omitted event lacked significance despite appreciating that it materially qualified the represented history. That requires separate evidence about knowledge and intention.

The repository should establish function first and motive only where evidence permits.

XII. Governance and restoration of the omitted event

Once the patient formally restores the omitted pre-closure event to the clinician and institution, the issue is no longer simply that the old chart does not contain it. The completeness and provenance of the chart are themselves under challenge.

Historical integrity does not require erasure. The original record can remain intact while later evidence, annotation or source-sensitive explanation changes the authority attached to it.

The ethical test is whether the system possesses a route by which omitted corrective information can regain force after it is expressly brought back into view.

XIII. The strongest innocent explanation

The patient had a difficult mixed presentation. Structural foot disease was real. Severe Parkinsonism was correctly recognised. A separate peripheral component was plausible. The neurologist investigated extensively, considered genetics and sought outside advice. The Protest may have been sincerely understood as a remaining podiatric concern rather than a challenge to the neurological formulation. Later treatment response can coexist with mixed mechanisms.

Nothing in the documentary architecture alone disproves that account.

XIV. The strongest concerning explanation

The competing possibility is that direct notice identified an unresolved founding relationship before closure, but the remaining opportunity produced more documentary settlement than discriminating inquiry. The resulting representation then governed later care; boundary-crossing evidence was largely accommodated locally; the patient remained continuously aware of the original objection; and later defence and governance operated substantially through the accumulated documentary state.

This explanation is more serious and requires correspondingly stronger proof, especially provenance evidence and evidence of knowledge where motive is alleged.

XV. The ethical core

The deepest issue is not simply whether somebody was wrong. It is whether corrective information remained capable of changing the system after it had been received.

There is one history. The ethical problem arises when the representation of that history becomes harder to correct than the reality it is meant to serve.
